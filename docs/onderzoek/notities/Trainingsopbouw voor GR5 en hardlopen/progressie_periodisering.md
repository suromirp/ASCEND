# Progressive overload & periodization for endurance and mountain (GR5) training

Evidence grades used per claim: **[Strong]** = multiple RCTs / meta-analysis with consistent result; **[Moderate]** = single large RCT or large prospective cohort, or meta-analysis of low-quality studies; **[Weak]** = small studies, observational, indirect/extrapolated; **[Expert]** = coaching/expert opinion, consensus without trial data.

Context for the report writer: the app currently progresses mostly by duration (e.g. easy run 30→35→40→27 min within a 4-week wave; +8% per repeated cycle). That is ~+17% (wk1→2) and ~+14% (wk2→3) week-on-week, with a ~33% deload.

## 1. Which variables to progress, and in what order ("one variable at a time")

### Takeaway
There is no trial evidence for a strict "one variable at a time" rule or a fixed order; it is a sound expert heuristic. The best-supported structure is: build easy aerobic volume first, keep intensity distribution mostly low, add specificity (load, D+, terrain) progressively in the later phases, and combine load carriage with strength + aerobic work rather than relying on load carriage alone.

### Cited Findings
- Combined programmes (whole-body strength + aerobic training + load-carriage sessions) produced the largest improvements in load-carriage performance in Knapik et al.'s (2012) systematic review **[Moderate]** — [Orr et al., Soldier Load Carriage review (PMC8069713)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8069713/); [Physical training considerations for load carriage, EJSS 2021](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/17461391.2021.1930193)
- Military guidance: training intensity of load carriage can be progressed via **speed of march, terrain grade, and terrain type** instead of mass; e.g. keep energy cost similar while increasing load mass by lowering march speed — i.e. trade one variable off against another **[Expert/Weak]** — [Orr et al. (PMC8069713)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8069713/)
- Uphill Athlete trekking model: first aerobic base without pack (weeks 1–4 bodyweight only), then add pack load stepwise; 6 days/week = 4 aerobic (Zone 1–2) + 2 strength + 1 rest; build from 5–8 h/week to 10–12+ h/week for demanding treks; minimum 8 weeks, optimal 16–24 weeks preparation **[Expert]** — [Uphill Athlete: Training for Trekking and Hiking](https://uphillathlete.com/trekking/training-for-trekking-and-hiking/)
- Uphill Athlete: "training frequency matters more than intensity" for trekking prep **[Expert]** — [Uphill Athlete trekking](https://uphillathlete.com/trekking/training-for-trekking-and-hiking/)
- Marathon performance (observational, recreational): >65 km/wk associated with faster finish times (β −14.1 min) vs <40 km/wk associated with slower (β +6.3 min); longest run <25 km associated with slower times (β +13.4 min); none of the training variables associated with new running-related injuries **[Weak–Moderate, observational]** — [Training for a (half-)marathon, Scand J Med Sci Sports (PMC7496388)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7496388/)

### Inferences
- Practical order supported by the above (expert-level synthesis): (1) frequency/consistency of easy aerobic sessions → (2) duration/volume (incl. long run / long hike) → (3) specificity: D+ per session, then pack mass, then terrain/back-to-back days → (4) intensity (hill intervals/threshold) as a minor share. Pack weight and D+ should not both jump in the same week (one-variable heuristic; [Expert]).
- For the app: progressing only duration ignores the specificity variables that matter most for GR5 (D+ per day, pack mass, consecutive days). Phase 3–4 ("mountain capacity", "expedition-ready") should shift progression to D+ and pack mass while holding duration roughly constant.

### Gaps
- No RCT found that tests "one variable at a time" vs simultaneous progression in endurance/hiking.
- No trial found on back-to-back long-day progression for multi-day trekking; only coaching practice.

## 2. Rate of increase: 10% rule, ACWR, single-session spikes, weekly D+ and pack weight

### Takeaway
The 10% weekly rule is not supported as an injury-prevention tool (Buist RCT: no difference). The strongest current signal is session-level: a single run longer than 110% of the longest run in the previous 30 days is associated with higher overuse-injury rates (Nielsen 2025, 5,205 runners). ACWR is heavily criticised and failed to predict injury in Nielsen 2025. No evidence-based weekly increment exists for D+; pack-weight progression guidance is expert/military practice.

### Cited Findings
- **Buist et al. (GRONORUN RCT, n=532 novices):** graded 13-week programme following the 10% rule vs standard 8-week programme → injury incidence 20.8% vs 20.3%, no difference **[Moderate]** — [PubMed 17940147](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17940147/)
  - Caveat: graded group increased weekly duration ~10.5%/wk vs ~23.7%/wk in standard group — both progressed at ≥10% **[Moderate]** — [Damsted et al. systematic review (PMC6253751)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6253751/)
- **Damsted et al. 2018 systematic review:** 3 of 4 eligible studies found an association between load increases and injury; e.g. novice half-marathon runners increasing weekly distance >30% vs <10% had HR 1.59; overall "very limited evidence" that sudden load change causes RRI **[Weak–Moderate]** — [PMC6253751](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6253751/)
- **Nielsen et al. 2025, BJSM** (prospective cohort, 5,205 runners from 87 countries, 18 months, 588,071 GPS-logged sessions, mean age 45.8; 1,311 overuse injuries). Spike = single-session distance vs longest run in previous 30 days **[Moderate]** — [PMC12421110](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12421110):
  - Small spike (>10–30%): HRR 1.64 (95% CI 1.31–2.05)
  - Moderate spike (>30–100%): HRR 1.52 (1.16–2.00)
  - Large spike (>100%): HRR 2.28 (1.50–3.48)
  - Reference: regression or ≤10% progression
  - Weekly ACWR: larger weekly spikes associated with *lower* injury rates (inverse); week-to-week ratio: no significant association
  - Authors: "Runners should avoid running a distance in their current session that exceeds 10% of the longest distance covered in the previous 30 days."
- **ACWR critiques:** coupled ACWR has mathematical coupling (acute load in numerator and denominator) creating spurious correlation ~0.50 between acute and chronic load (Lolli et al., BJSM) **[Moderate, methodological]** — [Lolli et al. (Semantic Scholar)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Mathematical-coupling-causes-spurious-correlation-Lolli-Batterham/82ac20419732a7838501199915536bb11bc66c7a); Impellizzeri et al. conclude there is no evidence supporting ACWR use in load-management systems or injury-reduction recommendations, and the time windows lack physiological justification — [Impellizzeri et al. 2020, "ACWR: Conceptual Issues and Fundamental Pitfalls"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/341936245_AcuteChronic_Workload_Ratio_Conceptual_Issues_and_Fundamental_Pitfalls); [JOSPT "A Cherry, Ripe for Picking"](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2021.9893)
- **Pack weight (expert/practical):**
  - Uphill Athlete: weeks 1–4 no pack; weeks 5–6 +5% bodyweight; weeks 7–8 +10% BW; weeks 9–10 +15–20% BW; cap at ~20% BW or the trek's actual pack weight; newer backpackers train with 10–15% less than expected trek load **[Expert]** — [Uphill Athlete trekking](https://uphillathlete.com/trekking/training-for-trekking-and-hiking/)
  - Military (Orr/Knapik): dedicated load-carriage sessions **no more than once every 10–14 days**; >4 sessions/month increase injury risk without performance gain; trunk/limb neuromuscular recovery after loaded marches takes 48–72 h; paper gives no kg increments **[Weak–Moderate, military populations, heavier loads]** — [PMC8069713](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8069713/)
  - Prior load-carriage injury is a strong risk factor: 52% of initially injured soldiers sustained further load-carriage injuries **[Moderate, military cohort]** — [PMC8069713](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8069713/)
  - Rucking community sources suggest ~5–10% weekly increase in weight×distance, or +1–2 kg every two weeks; these are non-peer-reviewed **[Expert/low quality]** — [Ruck Authority progression rules](https://ruckauthority.com/training/ruck-progression-rules-how-to-safely-increase-weight-and-dis); [Backpacker: loaded pack training](https://www.backpacker.com/skills/backpacking-fitness/get-stronger-and-faster-with-loaded-pack-training/)

### Inferences
- The current app wave (30→35→40 min = +17%, +14%) means the week-3 session is ~14% above the longest comparable session in 30 days → falls in Nielsen's "small spike" category (HRR 1.64). For the **long run** specifically this matters most; for short easy runs the absolute distances are small and the cohort did not separate session types. Suggested engine rule (extrapolated, [Moderate] for running distance): cap any session's distance/duration at ≤110% of the longest same-type session in the trailing 30 days. The app already has 28-day log windows for readiness, so this is computable from logs.
- Because weekly ACWR/week-to-week ratios did not predict injury in the largest cohort, the engine should not rely on ACWR; a per-session "max of last 30 days × 1.10" check is simpler and better supported.
- Extending the session-spike logic to **D+ per session** and **pack mass** is an extrapolation **[Weak/Expert]**: e.g. longest hike D+ ≤ +10–15% of the max in the past 30 days; pack +~2 kg steps (≈ +2–3% BW for a 70–80 kg person) at most every 1–2 weeks, and change pack mass and D+ in different weeks.
- For a 12 kg GR5 pack (~15–17% BW for 70–80 kg): the Uphill Athlete ladder reaches that in ~4–6 weeks of loaded hiking after a 4-week unloaded base — this fits phases 2–4 of a 16-week plan.
- Military "once per 10–14 days" refers to heavy, long loaded marches (soldier loads often much heavier than 12 kg); a recreational lighter-load hike weekly is likely fine but untested.

### Gaps
- **No peer-reviewed evidence found for a safe weekly increment of D+/elevation gain** (neither absolute metres nor %); only coaching practice. Downhill (eccentric) loading is a plausible injury driver but I found no progression data.
- Nielsen 2025 measured distance only (not duration, intensity, elevation, or pack load); applying the 10% session rule to duration-based prescriptions is an assumption.
- Gabbett's (2016) ACWR "sweet spot" (commonly cited as ~0.8–1.3) was not verified in a fetched source during this session; only the critiques were retrieved.
- No source found giving kg-based pack increments from controlled studies in civilians.

## 3. Intensity distribution: polarized vs pyramidal vs threshold; 80/20; place of tempo work

### Takeaway
Both polarized and pyramidal distributions (≈75–80% low intensity) are supported; differences between them are small. Polarized shows a small VO2peak advantage mainly in short (<12 wk) interventions and highly trained athletes, with no advantage on time-trial performance. Marathoners (elite) tend toward pyramidal, with substantial threshold/tempo work — the more relevant model for a marathon/GR5 recreational athlete.

### Cited Findings
- **Oliveira et al. 2024 meta-analysis** (17 studies, 437 participants, interventions 4–16 wk, median 10 wk) **[Moderate]** — [PMC11329428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11329428/):
  - Polarized > other TIDs for VO2peak: SMD 0.24 (95% CI 0.01–0.48), small effect
  - No difference for time trial (SMD −0.01), time to exhaustion (SMD 0.30, ns), speed/power at VT2/LT2 (SMD 0.04, ns)
  - VO2peak advantage only in interventions <12 weeks (SMD 0.40) and in highly trained/national-level athletes (SMD 0.46); no difference for ≥12-week interventions (p=0.83) or trained/developmental athletes (p=0.62)
  - Definitions: polarized = 75–80% Z1, 15–20% Z3, <10% Z2; pyramidal = most volume in Z1 with decreasing Z2 and Z3; threshold = emphasis on Z2
  - Limitations: incomplete TID reporting, uncontrolled volume differences, lab outcomes only
- **Casado et al. 2022 systematic review (IJSPP)** — elite/highly trained distance runners: marathoners used more pyramidal approaches, 1500 m runners more polarized; traditional periodization with hard-day/easy-day pattern **[Moderate, descriptive]** — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/359923898_Training_Periodization_Methods_Intensity_Distribution_and_Volume_in_Highly_Trained_and_Elite_Distance_Runners_A_Systematic_Review)
- Casado et al. (2019): world-class long-distance performance best predicted by volume of easy runs plus deliberate practice of short intervals and tempo runs **[Weak–Moderate, observational]** — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332769237_World-Class_Long-Distance_Running_Performances_Are_Best_Predicted_by_Volume_of_Easy_Runs_and_Deliberate_Practice_of_Short-Interval_and_Tempo_Runs)
- A 2026 RCT (Esteve-Lanao, Casado et al.) directly compared polarized vs pyramidal in recreational marathoners — [Int J Sports Sci Coach 2026](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/17479541261468168) (results not retrieved; see Gaps)
- A 16-week RCT in well-trained runners compared pyramidal vs polarized — [PMC9299127](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9299127/) (results not retrieved in detail)
- Practice summaries: polarized slight edge for 5–10 km/VO2max, pyramidal slight edge for marathon/lactate threshold; both beat threshold-heavy/unstructured training **[Expert/secondary]** — [Pheidi: polarized vs pyramidal](https://pheidi.training/articles/polarized-vs-pyramidal-training/)

### Inferences
- For this athlete (week: easy run, uphill hike, hill intervals, long run, recovery + 3 strength): ~80% of endurance time in Z1–Z2 (easy run, long run, uphill hiking, recovery) and one quality session (hill intervals) already approximates an 80/20 distribution. For marathon ambition, a pyramidal tweak — making part of the long run or a separate session tempo/threshold in the build phase — is consistent with the evidence ([Moderate] that either works; [Expert] on exact placement).
- Uphill hiking with pack tends to drift into Z2–Z3 because of gradient; heart-rate/RPE caps on hiking sessions keep the distribution intact (expert inference).

### Gaps
- No recreational-specific meta-analysis found; results of the 2026 recreational-marathoner RCT not retrieved.
- No trial found on intensity distribution for multi-day trekking/hiking specifically.
- Specific threshold/tempo progression schemes (e.g. 2×10 → 3×10 → 2×20 min) were not found in peer-reviewed sources in this session.

## 4. Hill interval and threshold/tempo progression

### Takeaway
Uphill intervals on ~10–15% gradient at ~100–110% of vVO2max improved 5-km performance ~2% over 6 weeks in well-trained runners (Barnes et al. 2013). Peer-reviewed data on how to progress rep count/duration/recovery over a mesocycle are essentially absent; progression schemes are coaching practice.

### Cited Findings
- Barnes, Hopkins, McGuigan & Kilding (2013, IJSPP): 20 well-trained runners randomised to 1 of 5 uphill interval programmes for 6 weeks (dose-response design) **[Weak–Moderate, small n]** — [PubMed 23538293](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23538293/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/258315513_Effects_of_Different_Uphill_Interval-Training_Programs_on_Running_Economy_and_Performance)
  - Reported (secondary summary): 10–15% gradient at 100–110% vVO2max improved 5-km TT by ~2% — [search summary of Barnes et al.](https://www.scienceofultra.com/s/Barnes-et-al_Effects-of-different-uphill-interval-training-programs-on-running-economy-and-performan.pdf)
- Uphill high-speed intervals affect muscle performance differently depending on slope (Theofilidis 2021) **[Weak]** — [PMC7785049](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7785049/)
- Military: grade and terrain are legitimate intensity levers for load carriage instead of more mass **[Expert]** — [PMC8069713](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8069713/)

### Inferences
- Expert-level progression logic (not trial-backed): within a phase, progress **total time at intensity** first (more reps, e.g. +1 rep/week), then rep duration, and only then reduce recovery or increase gradient — changing one of these per week. Deload week: ~half the reps.
- For GR5 specificity, later phases can convert hill intervals from running-speed (VO2max) work to longer sustained uphill efforts (e.g. threshold-paced climbs) — inference consistent with pyramidal/marathon evidence above.

### Gaps
- Could not extract the exact five Barnes protocols (PDF not parseable in this session); exact rep schemes should be verified before citing.
- No peer-reviewed progression model for hill rep count/duration/recovery over 16 weeks found.

## 5. Load-carriage progression (pack weight, frequency)

### Takeaway
Evidence (military) supports combining strength + aerobic + load-carriage training, with dedicated heavy load sessions only every 10–14 days and 48–72 h neuromuscular recovery. Civilian increments (≈5% BW steps every ~2 weeks up to the target pack, ~20% BW cap) are expert guidance.

### Cited Findings
- See section 2 for numbers: Knapik 2012 combined programme best; load-carriage sessions ≤ once per 10–14 days; >4/month adds injury risk without benefit; 48–72 h recovery; 52% re-injury rate after load-carriage injury — [PMC8069713](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8069713/)
- Uphill Athlete ladder: 0 → 5% → 10% → 15–20% BW in 2-week steps after 4 unloaded weeks; cap 20% BW or trek load; novices train 10–15% below expected load — [Uphill Athlete trekking](https://uphillathlete.com/trekking/training-for-trekking-and-hiking/)
- Load-carriage intensity can be raised via speed, grade, terrain rather than mass — [PMC8069713](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8069713/)

### Inferences
- For a 12 kg target: e.g. 4–6 kg (phase 2) → 8 kg → 10 kg (phase 3) → 12 kg (phase 4), each step held ≥2 weeks, and not raised in the same week that D+ or duration increases; deload weeks drop pack weight too. [Expert extrapolation]
- The weekly "uphill hiking session" can carry a light pack most weeks; the heaviest/longest loaded hike (the "load-carriage session" in military terms) every ~10–14 days.

### Gaps
- No controlled civilian trial on pack-mass increments; the 12 kg target is lighter than most military research loads, so frequency limits may be overly conservative.

## 6. Deload, periodization models, and tapering

### Takeaway
Block periodization shows small advantages over traditional periodization (low-quality studies); no strong evidence favours linear vs undulating for endurance. Deload weeks are widely recommended (e.g. ~50% volume reduction every 3rd–4th week) but direct evidence of benefit is thin; in a resistance-training trial a 1-week deload did not add gains. Tapering has the best evidence: ~2 weeks, volume −41–60%, keep intensity and frequency.

### Cited Findings
- **Mølmen et al. 2019 meta-analysis (block vs traditional):** small advantages for block on VO2max (SMD 0.40, 95% CI 0.02–0.79) and Wmax (SMD 0.28, 0.01–0.54); moderate–large effects for performance/threshold workloads; but small studies, mean PEDro 3.7/10 **[Weak–Moderate]** — [PubMed 31802956](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31802956)
- **Bosquet et al. 2007 meta-analysis (27 studies):** most effective taper = ~2 weeks, training volume reduced exponentially by 41–60%, without altering intensity or frequency **[Strong for the taper principle]** — [Bosquet summary, SDSU coaching science](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol131/bosquet.htm); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Effects-of-tapering-on-performance:-a-Bosquet-Montpetit/a41517ab5fa06b92568b861e2b1aa32b3003d214)
- Endurance-specific taper meta-analysis (2023) also available — [PLOS One 2023](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0282838) (not fetched in detail)
- **Deload definition (Bell et al. 2023 Delphi consensus):** "a period of reduced training stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue, promote recovery, and enhance preparedness for the subsequent training cycle" **[Expert consensus]** — [Bell et al., Practical approach to deloading](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/35313/3/Bell-APracticalApproach(AM).pdf)
- **Coleman et al. 2024 (resistance training, 9 weeks):** a 1-week mid-programme deload produced no difference in hypertrophy, power or local endurance, and somewhat smaller strength gains vs continuous training **[Weak, RT not endurance]** — [PMC10809978](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10809978/)
- Uphill Athlete: every 3rd or 4th week reduce total training time by ~50%; pre-trek taper final 1–2 weeks with reduced volume and strength at ~50% **[Expert]** — [Uphill Athlete trekking](https://uphillathlete.com/trekking/training-for-trekking-and-hiking/)

### Inferences
- The app's 3:1 wave (easy/build/hardest/deload) is consistent with expert practice; evidence it is superior to 2:1 or continuous loading is lacking — fine to keep, but deload magnitude in the app (~−33% vs peak) is milder than Uphill Athlete's ~−50% (both defensible).
- Before the GR5 departure (or a marathon), the final phase's last 1–2 weeks should be a Bosquet-style taper (volume −40–60%, keep some intensity and frequency) rather than a standard deload. For a multi-week trek, the "event" is itself a long load block, so arriving fresh matters more than peak fitness (inference).
- Phase design (base → build → mountain capacity → expedition-ready) resembles a block/traditional hybrid: emphasis shifts from general volume to specific (D+, pack) — consistent with Mølmen's weak support for concentrating emphases.

### Gaps
- No endurance RCT found testing deload weeks vs no deload for recreational runners/hikers.
- No evidence found comparing linear vs undulating periodization for recreational endurance athletes (search budget did not cover this).

## 7. Long-run progression for the marathon

### Takeaway
Longest run should progress so that any single long run stays ≤ ~10% beyond the longest run of the previous 30 days (Nielsen 2025). Observationally, marathoners whose longest run was <25 km were slower; 30–35 km longest runs were associated with better times with no extra benefit beyond 35 km. No peer-reviewed evidence was found for the popular "long run ≤ 25–30% of weekly volume" rule.

### Cited Findings
- Single-session spike >10% vs longest run of last 30 days → HRR 1.64; >100% → HRR 2.28 **[Moderate]** — [Nielsen et al. 2025 (PMC12421110)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12421110)
- Marathon: longest run <25 km associated with slower finish (β +13.4 min, 95% CI 5.3–21.6); >35 km gave no additional benefit vs 30–35 km; weekly volume >65 km/wk associated with faster times; no training variable associated with injury **[Weak–Moderate, observational]** — [PMC7496388](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7496388/)
- Half marathon: longest run >21 km associated with faster finish (β −3.9 min); >32 km/wk associated with faster times — [PMC7496388](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7496388/)

### Inferences
- Applying the Nielsen rule to a long run: e.g. 14 km → 15.4 → ~17 → ~18.5 → ~20 → 22 → 24 → 26.5 → 29 → 32 km takes ~9–10 progression steps; with 3:1 waves (deload resets don't reduce the 30-day max), reaching 30–32 km from ~14 km takes roughly 12–16 weeks — i.e. a marathon is realistic in a second 16-week cycle unless the base long run is already ≥18–20 km (inference).
- The "+8% per repeated cycle" in the app is consistent with the 10% session rule only if the within-wave step to the hardest week also stays ≤10% above the trailing 30-day max — currently it does not (wk2→wk3 +14%). Suggested fix: flatten the wave (e.g. 100% → 105% → 110% → 70%) or derive the hardest-week target from the trailing-30-day max ×1.10.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evidence found for "long run max 25–30% (or 30–40%) of weekly volume"; treat as coaching convention [Expert] if the report writer uses it.
- Nielsen 2025 did not distinguish long runs from other runs, nor trail vs road.
