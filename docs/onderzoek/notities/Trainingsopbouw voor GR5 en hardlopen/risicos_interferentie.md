# Risks, downsides and trade-offs: GR5 trek prep + running + strength (recreational adult, 16 weeks)

Evidence grade legend used per claim: **[Strong]** = consistent systematic reviews/meta-analyses of RCTs or large prospective cohorts; **[Moderate]** = single large prospective cohort, meta-analysis with notable heterogeneity, or consistent smaller RCTs; **[Weak]** = small/lab studies, low-quality reviews, indirect populations (e.g. military recruits, adolescents); **[Expert opinion]** = consensus/narrative recommendation without direct outcome data.

Context reminder for the writer: week = 2 upper + 1 heavy lower strength, easy run day after heavy legs, uphill hiking session, hill intervals Sat, long run Sun on tired legs, recovery Mon; target GR5 ~600 km, ~12 kg pack, ~30,000 m D+/D−; marathon ambition. App rule: heavy sessions for same muscle group ~48 h apart.

## 1. Running injuries: incidence, common diagnoses, risk factors, role of load errors

### Takeaway
Running injuries are common (roughly 7.7 injuries per 1000 h for recreational runners, ~18 per 1000 h for novices), dominated by knee, Achilles/lower-leg and foot overuse problems. Previous injury and training volume/structure are the most consistently implicated risk factors; the strongest recent data show that injuries are triggered by *single-session spikes* in distance (>10% above the longest run of the previous 30 days), not only by weekly volume.

### Cited Findings
- **[Strong]** Meta-analysis (Videbæk et al. 2015, *Sports Med* 45:1017–1026): weighted incidence 17.8 injuries/1000 h (95% CI 16.7–19.1) in novice runners vs 7.7/1000 h (95% CI 6.9–8.7) in recreational runners; range across studies 2.5 (track/long-distance athletes) to 33.0 (novices) — [Videbæk 2015, Sports Med](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-015-0333-8)
- **[Moderate]** Systematic review (Kakouris, Yener & Fong 2021, *J Sport Health Sci*): pathologies with highest incidence proportions were anterior compartment tendinopathy (19.4%), patellofemoral pain syndrome (15.8%) and Achilles tendinopathy (13.7%); injury location proportions did not differ significantly between ultramarathoners and non-ultra runners. Authors stress methodological heterogeneity limits interpretation — [Kakouris 2021 (ResearchGate full text)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/350949028_A_systematic_review_of_running-related_musculoskeletal_injuries_in_runners/fulltext/617734c93c987366c3e9aa61/A-systematic-review-of-running-related-musculoskeletal-injuries-in-runners.pdf); [ScienceDirect record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2095254621000454)
- **[Moderate, but from low-quality reviews]** Umbrella review (Correia et al. 2024, *J Sport Health Sci* 13(6):793–804): running/training characteristics (weekly distance/volume), previous injury, race/marathon participation and hip/pelvic stability deficits showed large effect sizes for running-related injury; height, alcohol, participation in other sports showed medium effects; biomechanics (foot strike etc.) and BMI were limited/conflicting. Crucially, all 13 included reviews were rated critically low (8/13) or low (5/13) quality, and most analysed "any running injury" rather than specific diagnoses (MTSS, Achilles, PFP, plantar fasciitis) — [Correia 2024, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11336318/)
- **[Moderate–Strong; large prospective cohort]** Garmin-RUNSAFE (Frandsen et al. 2025, *BJSM* 59:1203–1210), 5,205 adult runners followed 18 months with GPS-watch data: when a single session's distance exceeded the longest run of the previous 30 days by >10%, overuse injury rate rose: small spike HRR 1.64 (95% CI 1.31–2.05), moderate spike HRR 1.52 (1.16–2.00), large spike HRR 2.28 (1.50–3.48). Authors conclude overuse injuries often arise "suddenly", from a single session, rather than gradually — [Frandsen 2025, SDU full text PDF](https://findresearcher.sdu.dk/ws/files/295073070/1203.full.pdf); [Aarhus University record](https://pure.au.dk/portal/en/publications/how-much-running-is-too-much-identifying-high-risk-running-sessio/); [MedicalXpress summary](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2025-07-injuries-suddenly-gradually-overuse.html)
- **[Weak–Moderate]** Prospective-study meta-analysis of biomechanical/musculoskeletal measures as risk factors in non-elite runners exists (Sports Medicine – Open 2022), consistent with the umbrella review finding that biomechanical predictors are weak/inconsistent — [Sports Med Open 2022](https://sportsmedicine-open.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40798-022-00416-z)

### Inferences
- The Sunday long run is the session most likely to create a "session spike" in a marathon build; the app could check each planned long run against the longest run in the previous 30 days and flag >10% increases (direct application of Frandsen 2025). This is a more evidence-aligned rule than the classic "10% per week" rule.
- Long hiking days with pack are not "running distance", but they load the same tissues (Achilles, plantar fascia, tibia, knee). A comparable "longest-day-in-30-days" check for hiking time/D+ is a reasonable extrapolation (not directly tested).
- Previous injury is the most robust individual risk factor; onboarding should ask for injury history and bias towards slower progression if present.
- Diagnosis-specific risk factors (e.g. for MTSS, plantar fasciitis) remain poorly established; prevention should therefore rely on general load management and strength rather than diagnosis-specific screening.

### Gaps
- Diagnosis-specific incidence figures (e.g. Lopes 2012 values for MTSS, plantar fasciitis) and diagnosis-specific risk factors for bone stress injury in recreational runners were not retrieved in this session.
- No study found that combines running injury incidence with concurrent heavy hiking/load carriage in recreational adults.

## 2. Downhill/descending: knee and quad load, eccentric damage, repeated bout effect, trekking poles

### Takeaway
Descending (~30,000 m D− on GR5) is the dominant source of muscle damage and knee load. Downhill running/walking causes marked, multi-day strength loss, but the repeated bout effect is powerful: prior, progressive downhill exposure is the single most effective protective strategy. Trekking poles reduce knee joint forces (~12–25%) and post-trek muscle damage.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate; narrative review of lab studies]** Bontemps et al. 2020 (*Sports Med*) "Downhill running: what are the effects and how can we adapt?": after downhill running (median protocol −12% slope, 40 min) knee-extensor isometric MVC drops −14 to −55% and plantar flexors −15 to −25%, with up to 4–5 days before full recovery; CK peaks ~24 h post (+346% untrained, +371% trained); downhill running shows larger impact and braking forces than level running — [Bontemps 2020, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7674385/)
- **[Moderate]** Same review: repeating downhill running after several days/weeks reduces CK, DOMS and speeds MVC recovery (repeated bout effect); the protection disappeared after 9 weeks without eccentric exposure; greater initial volume (e.g. multiple sessions/week for 1–2 weeks) gives larger protection; "prior exposure to DR is the most effective strategy to limit the magnitude of EIMD"; a "principle of progressivity" must be applied to avoid maladaptation/injury — [Bontemps 2020, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7674385/)
- **[Weak–Moderate]** Low-intensity (non-damaging) downhill walking can precondition against later damaging downhill walking; protection from low-intensity eccentric bouts reported to last ~2 weeks but not 3 weeks (Nosaka/Chen line of work) — [PLOS One 2017, non-damaging downhill walking](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0173909); [Appl Physiol Nutr Metab 2014, preconditioning before downhill walking](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/10.1139/apnm-2014-0390)
- **[Weak; small lab study]** Schwameder et al. 1999 (*J Sports Sci*): hiking poles reduced knee joint forces during downhill walking, mainly via force transferred to the poles and a more forward-leaning trunk shortening the knee moment arm; secondary sources report ~12–25% reduction in knee force on steep descents — [Schwameder 1999 (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/12692643_Knee_joint_forces_during_downhill_walking_with_hiking_poles)
- **[Weak; small RCT]** Howatson et al. (trekking poles during a mountain walk): pole group had attenuated MVC loss immediately, 24 and 48 h post-trek, lower soreness at 24 and 48 h, and lower CK at 24 h — [Howatson, "Trekking poles reduce exercise-induced muscle injury during mountain walking"](https://www.academia.edu/18237927/Trekking_Poles_Reduce_Exercise_Induced_Muscle_Injury_during_Mountain_Walking)
- **[Weak]** Trekking poles also reduced downhill-walking induced muscle and cartilage damage markers in obese women (Cho/Roh) — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Trekking-poles-reduce-downhill-walking-induced-and-Cho-Roh/68f243c30a51a2fb6de28a6a4836bd6bb008edfd)
- **[Expert opinion/narrative review]** Hawke & Jensen 2020 (*Wilderness Environ Med*) review "Are trekking poles helping or hindering your hiking experience?" summarises pole benefits and trade-offs — [Hawke & Jensen 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1016/j.wem.2020.06.009)

### Inferences
- Because the GR5 is mostly descent-limited (quad/knee), the 16-week plan should deliberately include *progressive downhill* exposure (hiking with poles and, carefully, downhill running), not only uphill sessions. The current week template emphasises uphill hiking and hill intervals; uphill-only work gives little eccentric protection.
- Because RBE fades over weeks without eccentric loading, downhill exposure should be maintained at least every 1–2 weeks through to the taper (inference from 2- to 9-week decay data).
- Hill interval sessions: if they include fast descents/jog-backs, they add eccentric stress; the first exposures should be short and slow.
- Poles: recommend practising with poles in training (with the pack) well before the trek — effect sizes are moderate but consistent and the cost is low.
- Heavy lower-body strength (eccentric squats/lunges/step-downs) likely also provides some cross-transfer to eccentric tolerance (plausible, not directly evidenced in this search).

### Gaps
- No direct data on how much descent per week a recreational trekker can safely add, nor on cumulative D− over multi-day trekking and patellofemoral pain incidence.
- Pole studies are small (n≈10–40), mostly single-session; long-term injury prevention with poles not demonstrated.

## 3. Load carriage risks (back pain, blisters, stress fractures, metatarsalgia) and safe progression

### Takeaway
Military load-carriage literature consistently lists foot blisters as the most common injury, followed by lower-limb overuse (stress fractures, knee and foot pain, metatarsalgia), back strain and rucksack palsy. Recommended conditioning: regular, progressive load marching (at least twice a month, weekly appears better), increasing distance and load *not at the same time*, with built-in recovery and supplemented by strength + aerobic training.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate; narrative reviews of military cohorts]** Knapik et al. 2004 (*Mil Med*) "Soldier load carriage: historical, physiological, biomechanical, and medical aspects" — review documenting injuries associated with load carriage — [PubMed 14964502](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14964502/)
- **[Moderate]** Load carriage injuries range from blisters, low back injuries and knee/foot pain to stress fractures and brachial plexus palsy; lower limbs are the most frequent site; foot blisters are the most common, friction-related in boots on long marches; also metatarsalgia, digitalgia, meralgia paresthetica, rucksack palsy — [Orr et al., Soldier Load Carriage, Injuries, Rehabilitation and Physical Conditioning (IJERPH 2021)](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/18/8/4010); [Orr, narrative review of load-carriage injuries (Bond)](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/soldier-occupational-load-carriage-a-narrative-review-of-associat/); [Knapik, Injuries and injury prevention during foot marching](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/268391989_Injuries_and_injury_prevention_during_foot_marching)
- **[Expert opinion/Weak]** Orr et al. narrative review (JMVH) on conditioning: Knapik (1993, 2004) recommended load marching "at least two times a month"; Visser et al. (1995) found weekly superior to fortnightly; Dutch military used a ~10-day load-carriage conditioning cycle; initial loads light and progressing; task durations/distances "gradually increase (yet not at the same time as increase in load)"; higher load/lower distance groups improved more but sustained high-intensity load carriage carries injury risk; need recovery periods spread through the program; continuous high volumes of vigorous weight-bearing activity raise injury rates — [Orr et al., JMVH narrative review](https://jmvh.org/article/load-carriage-minimising-soldier-injuries-through-physical-conditioning-a-narrative-review-2/)
- **[Weak]** Knapik et al. 1990 tested 0, 1, 2, 4 load-carriage sessions/month (18–34 kg, up to 16 km per session) over 9 weeks — [Orr et al. 2021 IJERPH PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/1558/e39873f22bc17355a0889c9f8b86e906617e.pdf)
- **[Weak]** Rudzki (1989) progressed walking speed 5→7.5 km/h and loads from ~16–21 kg to ~24–29 kg effectively — [JMVH review](https://jmvh.org/article/load-carriage-minimising-soldier-injuries-through-physical-conditioning-a-narrative-review-2/)
- **[Moderate]** Combined resistance + aerobic conditioning improved loaded (44.7 kg) 3.2 km performance (Kraemer 2001, 2004), though load-carriage-specific training should stay the focal point — [JMVH review](https://jmvh.org/article/load-carriage-minimising-soldier-injuries-through-physical-conditioning-a-narrative-review-2/)
- **[Weak]** A progressive load-carriage program in US Marine Corps recruit training has been implemented and its injuries profiled (Kelly, Orr et al. 2024, *Work*) — [Kelly 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3233/WOR-230569)

### Inferences
- A 12 kg pack is light compared with military loads (18–45 kg), so absolute risk is lower, but the GR5's *duration* (many consecutive days) resembles the high-volume continuous weight-bearing that the military literature associates with higher injury rates.
- Practical progression for the app: weekly pack hike; increase either duration/D+ or pack weight in a given week, never both; start at ~5–8 kg (inference, not a published figure for civilians) and reach full 12 kg in the last 4–6 weeks with full-day back-to-back simulations.
- Blister prevention (broken-in footwear, sock systems, taping hotspots) should be practised in training — blisters are the most likely injury and can end a trek even though they are "minor".
- Stress fracture risk factors overlap with running (sudden volume increases, low energy availability) — see sections 1 and 7.

### Gaps
- Military data come from young recruits under heavier loads and compulsory schedules; direct extrapolation to a recreational civilian with 12 kg is uncertain. No civilian trekker injury-rate data with quantified pack load were retrieved.
- Specific military incidence numbers for blisters/stress fractures were not extracted in this session.

## 4. Too-fast D+ build-up

### Takeaway
No study directly quantifies safe weekly increases in elevation gain/loss for hikers. Indirect evidence (session-spike data from running, multi-day strength loss after downhill exercise, and the repeated bout effect) supports progressive, staged exposure with particular caution for descent.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate]** Single-session spikes >10% above the longest session of the prior 30 days raise overuse injury rates in runners (HRR 1.52–2.28) — [Frandsen 2025 BJSM](https://findresearcher.sdu.dk/ws/files/295073070/1203.full.pdf)
- **[Moderate]** Unaccustomed downhill exercise causes up to 55% strength loss in knee extensors with up to 4–5 days to recover; a "principle of progressivity" is recommended — [Bontemps 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7674385/)
- **[Expert opinion]** Military reviews: increase distance/duration and load gradually and not simultaneously — [JMVH review](https://jmvh.org/article/load-carriage-minimising-soldier-injuries-through-physical-conditioning-a-narrative-review-2/)

### Inferences
- Applying the Frandsen logic to D+/D−: flag any session whose D+ or D− exceeds the largest of the preceding 30 days by more than ~10–20% (inference; threshold not validated for elevation).
- Too-fast D+ build-up primarily risks: quad DOMS and strength loss that compromise the following days' runs (interference with the Sunday long run), patellofemoral pain, Achilles/calf overload on steep ascents, and fatigue-related falls on descents.

### Gaps
- No validated threshold for weekly D+ or D− progression in recreational hikers found. This is a genuine evidence gap; any app rule here is expert opinion.

## 5. Interference effect (concurrent strength + endurance): relevance for recreational athletes, order and timing

### Takeaway
For recreational athletes, the interference effect is small: concurrent training does not meaningfully blunt maximal strength or hypertrophy (Schumann 2022), but explosive strength/power is attenuated, especially when both are done in the same session. Running causes more interference than cycling (Wilson 2012). Separating heavy lower-body strength from hard running by ≥6 h, ideally ~24 h, and doing strength first when same-day, minimises interference.

### Cited Findings
- **[Strong]** Schumann et al. 2022 (*Sports Med*), updated meta-analysis of 43 studies: concurrent aerobic + strength training did not compromise hypertrophy (SMD −0.01, 95% CI −0.16 to 0.18) or maximal strength vs strength alone, across ages, training status and endurance modes; explosive strength gains were attenuated, particularly when both were performed in the same session — [Schumann 2022, Sports Med](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7); [Stronger by Science summary](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/research-spotlight-interference-effect/)
- **[Moderate]** Wilson et al. 2012 (*JSCR* 26(8):2293–2307), 21 studies: concurrent training with *running* (not cycling) significantly reduced strength and hypertrophy gains vs strength alone; proposed mechanism: running's eccentric/impact-related muscle damage and higher metabolic stress — [Wilson 2012, JSCR](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2012/08000/concurrent_training__a_meta_analysis_examining.35.aspx)
- **[Moderate]** Meta-analysis on sex and training status (Huiberts et al. 2023, *Sports Med*): reported no significant interference on strength in untrained individuals, and no interference in trained individuals when sessions were separated — [Sports Med 2023, sex & training status](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-023-01943-9) (attribution of this specific statement based on search summary; verify in full text)
- **[Moderate]** Eddens, van Someren & Howatson 2018 (*Sports Med*) meta-analysis: strength before endurance in the same session was more beneficial for lower-body dynamic strength than the reverse — [Concurrent training review (Hughes 2022, Univ. Gloucestershire)](https://eprints.glos.ac.uk/10611/9/10611-Hughes-(2022)-Concurrent-training.pdf); [Medicine 2024 review on optimizing concurrent programs](https://journals.lww.com/md-journal/fulltext/2024/12270/optimizing_concurrent_training_programs__a_review.22.aspx)
- **[Weak–Moderate; single RCT]** Robineau et al. 2016: strength/power adaptations were inhibited unless ≥6 h separated strength and high-intensity endurance; 24 h recovery was superior for reducing interference — [Medicine 2024 review](https://journals.lww.com/md-journal/fulltext/2024/12270/optimizing_concurrent_training_programs__a_review.22.aspx)
- **[Expert opinion]** Suggested strategies to limit interference: 6–24 h between modes, endurance ≤3 days/week when maximising strength, cycling instead of running where possible, post-exercise nutrition — [Medicine 2024 review](https://journals.lww.com/md-journal/fulltext/2024/12270/optimizing_concurrent_training_programs__a_review.22.aspx)
- **[Strong]** Strength training does not reduce VO2max/lactate markers in runners (Blagrove 2018) — i.e. interference in the endurance direction is not observed — [Blagrove 2018, Sports Med](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-017-0835-7)

### Inferences
- For this athlete, whose goal is endurance (trek + marathon), interference on strength/hypertrophy is largely irrelevant; the real concern is *residual fatigue/muscle damage* from the heavy lower session compromising the quality or safety of the next running/hiking sessions, and vice versa.
- The current plan (easy run the day after heavy legs) is consistent with the evidence: ~24 h gap, easy intensity. Placing heavy legs ≥48 h before Saturday hill intervals and the Sunday long run protects key sessions. Heavy legs immediately after (or on the day before) a big descent day should be avoided.
- If strength and running must be on the same day, do strength first, then the run ≥6 h later (Eddens/Robineau).
- In the last 2–3 weeks before departure and during the trek, reduce heavy lower-body volume (maintain with low volume) — expert-opinion taper logic.
- The 48 h same-muscle-group rule in the app is reasonable for heavy strength; it should treat long downhill hikes and hill intervals as lower-body "heavy" loads too (already partially done via the leg-heavy rule).

### Gaps
- Hickson (1980), the original interference study, was not retrieved in this session; it is historically important but involved very high training frequencies (strength 5 d/wk + endurance 6 d/wk) in untrained subjects.
- Little data on interference with *hiking with load* as the endurance mode.

## 6. Back-to-back long days (Saturday hill intervals + Sunday long run on tired legs)

### Takeaway
No controlled studies were found on back-to-back long sessions in recreational runners/trekkers. The practice is grounded in specificity (the GR5 is consecutive days of loaded walking) and is widely used in ultra/trek preparation, but it concentrates load and fatigue, the setting in which session-spike injuries occur.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate, indirect]** Overuse injuries are linked to single-session spikes rather than slow accumulation — relevant because a long run on pre-fatigued legs is the highest-load session of the week — [Frandsen 2025 BJSM](https://findresearcher.sdu.dk/ws/files/295073070/1203.full.pdf)
- **[Moderate, indirect]** Downhill/eccentric loading causes force loss lasting up to 4–5 days, so a Saturday hill session with descents will leave reduced strength on Sunday — [Bontemps 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7674385/)
- **[Weak]** In ultramarathon runners, training load–injury relationships have been modelled with generalised additive models (2025) — [PubMed 40772114](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40772114/)
- **[Expert opinion]** Military reviews warn that continuous high volumes of vigorous weight-bearing activity increase injury rates and require recovery periods — [JMVH review](https://jmvh.org/article/load-carriage-minimising-soldier-injuries-through-physical-conditioning-a-narrative-review-2/)

### Inferences
- Benefits (expert-opinion level): specificity for multi-day trekking, fatigue-resistance practice, fuelling/footcare rehearsal, psychological confidence.
- Risks: compounding of the two hardest sessions, running mechanics degrading with fatigue, higher session-spike likelihood.
- Mitigations: keep the Sunday run easy-paced; progress the Sunday long run conservatively against the 30-day longest run; use a recovery/deload week every 3–4 weeks (expert opinion); consider replacing some back-to-back run weekends with back-to-back *pack hikes* closer to the trek to be more specific with less impact.
- Monday recovery placement is appropriate.

### Gaps
- No RCT or cohort specifically evaluating back-to-back long-session weekends in recreational endurance athletes was found.

## 7. Recovery, sleep, energy availability (REDs), nutrition (brief)

### Takeaway
Short sleep is associated with higher injury risk (mostly adolescent data); low energy availability (REDs) impairs health and performance and is linked to bone stress injury. With high weekly volume plus possible deliberate weight management via MacroFactor, energy availability is a relevant risk.

### Cited Findings
- **[Weak–Moderate; adolescent data]** Milewski et al. 2014: adolescent athletes sleeping <8 h/night were 1.7 times more likely to be injured — [Milewski 2014 (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/263971781_Chronic_Lack_of_Sleep_is_Associated_With_Increased_Sports_Injuries_in_Adolescent_Athletes); [Sleep and the Young Athlete, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10293556/)
- **[Weak–Moderate]** Meta-analysis in adolescents: chronic <8 h sleep associated with musculoskeletal injury, OR 1.58 (95% CI 1.05–2.37) — [ResearchGate meta-analysis](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332086833_CHRONIC_LACK_OF_SLEEP_IS_ASSOCIATED_WITH_INCREASED_SPORTS_INJURY_IN_ADOLESCENTS_A_SYSTEMATIC_REVIEW_AND_META-ANALYSIS); a 2025 preprint meta-analysis in athletes reported shorter sleep associated with higher injury risk, OR 1.34 (1.08–1.66) (preprint, not peer reviewed) — [Research Square preprint](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-7606280/v1)
- **[Expert opinion/Consensus]** 2023 IOC consensus on REDs (Mountjoy et al., *BJSM*): low energy availability lies on a spectrum from adaptable to problematic; introduces REDs CAT2 clinical tool; REDs linked to adverse health and performance outcomes including bone stress injuries — [IOC REDs consensus 2023 PDF](https://stillmed.olympics.com/media/Documents/Athletes/Medical-Scientific/Consensus-Statements/REDs/BJSM-IOC-consensus-statement-on-Relative-Energy-Deficiency-in-Sport-REDs.pdf); [Wu Tsai summary](https://humanperformancealliance.org/news/2023-international-olympic-committee-consensus-statement-on-reds/)
- **[Expert opinion]** The REDs concept/syndrome is debated in the literature — [Does REDs syndrome exist? PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11561064/)

### Inferences
- If MacroFactor is used for a calorie deficit during a 16-week high-volume build, energy availability risk rises; recommend at most a modest deficit, none in peak/back-to-back weeks, and adequate carbohydrate around long sessions (expert opinion consistent with IOC consensus).
- Warning signs worth surfacing in the app (expert opinion): persistent fatigue, declining performance, disturbed sleep, recurrent illness, menstrual disturbances (women), bone pain.
- Sleep target ≥7–8 h is a cheap, plausible mitigation; evidence in adults is weaker than in adolescents.

### Gaps
- Adult recreational-athlete sleep–injury data are sparse; most evidence is adolescent.
- Prevalence of low energy availability in recreational male/female hikers/runners not retrieved.

## 8. Pros: what each element contributes

### Takeaway
Strength training is one of the best-supported elements: it improves running economy (2–8%) without harming aerobic markers and, across sports, strength-based programmes reduce injuries substantially (RR ~0.32 overall, overuse injuries roughly halved). Load-specific hiking develops the tissue tolerance and skills the trek requires; progressive downhill exposure provides protective adaptation.

### Cited Findings
- **[Strong]** Blagrove, Howatson & Hayes 2018 (*Sports Med* 48:1117–1149), 24 studies, 469 trained distance runners: 6–20 weeks of strength training (heavy, explosive or plyometric, 1–4×/wk) improved running economy by 2–8%, without negative effects on VO2max or lactate markers — [Blagrove 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-017-0835-7)
- **[Moderate]** Later meta-analyses on strength training in middle/long-distance runners and on heavy resistance vs plyometric training report improvements in running economy and time-trial performance — [Sports Med 2023, RE at different speeds](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-023-01978-y); [Sports Med 2024, performance](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-024-02018-z); [Sports Med Open 2022, heavy vs plyometric](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-022-00511-1)
- **[Moderate–Strong; not runner-specific]** Lauersen, Bertelsen & Andersen 2014 (*BJSM* 48:871–877), 25 RCTs, 26,610 participants, 3,464 injuries: strength training RR 0.315 (95% CI 0.207–0.480) — injuries reduced to less than one-third; overuse injuries reduced by almost half; stretching showed no protective effect. Note: only 4 strength trials, mostly team/other sports, not runners — [Lauersen 2014 BJSM PDF](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/55b7ffebe4b0568a75e3316b/t/58331e36e58c627c2abd00b5/1479745080809/Br+J+Sports+Med-2014-Lauersen-871-7.pdf)
- **[Moderate]** Combined resistance + aerobic training improved loaded-march performance in soldiers (Kraemer 2001, 2004) — [JMVH review](https://jmvh.org/article/load-carriage-minimising-soldier-injuries-through-physical-conditioning-a-narrative-review-2/)
- **[Moderate]** Prior downhill exposure is the most effective strategy to limit exercise-induced muscle damage — [Bontemps 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7674385/)

### Inferences
- Retaining 3 strength sessions is well justified; for the endurance goal, the single heavy lower session is the most valuable for injury resilience and running economy, while upper body supports pack carriage and posture (upper-body/trunk benefit for load carriage is plausible, not directly quantified here).
- Trade-off: marathon ambition and GR5 preparation pull in slightly different directions (running volume/impact vs loaded walking/eccentric descent); in a 16-week window, the athlete should pick a primary goal for the final 4–6 weeks. If the GR5 is the primary goal, running volume should plateau while pack-hiking volume rises.

### Gaps
- Injury-prevention RCTs of strength training specifically in recreational runners are fewer and less conclusive than Lauersen's cross-sport estimate suggests; not retrieved in detail in this session.

| Single-session spike in long-run distance | Moderate–Strong (Frandsen 2025) | Keep each long run ≤10% above longest run in previous 30 days |
| Overuse running injury (knee, Achilles, shin, foot) | Strong (incidence); risk factors low-quality reviews | Gradual volume, strength training, respect previous injury |
| Downhill quad damage / knee load | Moderate (lab) | Progressive downhill exposure every 1–2 weeks; poles; eccentric strength |
| Blisters, foot pain, stress fractures with pack | Moderate (military) | Weekly pack hike; progress load OR distance, not both; footwear/sock rehearsal |
| Too-fast D+/D− build-up | Gap / expert opinion | Track D+/D− per session against 30-day max; conservative increases |
| Interference strength ↔ endurance | Strong (small effect on strength/size), moderate (power) | ≥6 h, ideally 24 h, between heavy legs and hard runs; strength first if same day |
| Back-to-back long days | Expert opinion | Easy-paced Sunday; deload every 3–4 weeks; shift to back-to-back pack hikes near trek |
| Low energy availability (REDs) | Consensus/expert | Avoid significant deficit in high-load weeks; monitor warning signs |
| Short sleep | Weak–Moderate (adolescent data) | ≥7–8 h sleep; treat sleep loss as a readiness reducer |
