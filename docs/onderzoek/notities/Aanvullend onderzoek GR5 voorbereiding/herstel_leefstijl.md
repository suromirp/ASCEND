# Recovery & lifestyle factors for a recreational concurrent-training athlete (16-week GR5 trek + marathon build)

Scope: recreational adult, strength 3x/week (MacroFactor-tracked nutrition, sometimes in a calorie deficit), running + mountain hiking, occasional 1–2 beers on Thursday. Evidence grades used per claim: **[Strong]** = meta-analysis/systematic review or position stand built on consistent RCTs; **[Moderate]** = smaller meta-analysis, consistent RCTs, or large observational data; **[Weak]** = single studies, extrapolation from different populations/doses; **[Expert]** = consensus statement opinion or coaching practice without direct trial data.

Research-process note: the full texts of the 2023 IOC REDs consensus and the 2022/23 IOC acute-respiratory-illness consensus could not be fetched (HTTP 503/403, PDF not parseable in this environment). Findings for those topics come from search-result abstracts/secondary summaries and are flagged accordingly.

## 1. Sleep: how much, effect of short sleep on performance and injury, practical rules

### Takeaway
A single night of ≤6 h sleep measurably lowers next-day performance (~7–8% on average across strength, power, endurance and skill), with early waking ("late restriction") and total deprivation having the most consistent effect. Expert consensus recommends individualising sleep need rather than a fixed 7–9 h, but chronic <8 h is associated with ~1.7x injury risk in (adolescent) athletes.

### Cited Findings
- **[Strong]** Meta-analysis of 69 studies (adults, sleep loss defined as ≤6 h in any 24 h): overall effect of acute sleep loss on exercise performance −7.56% (95% CI −11.9 to −3.13%). Strength, anaerobic power, endurance and skill were all negatively affected. — [Craven et al. 2022, Sports Medicine](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-022-01706-y)
- **[Strong]** In that meta-analysis, total deprivation and "late restriction" (waking earlier than preferred) were the only sleep-loss types with consistent negative effects; early restriction (going to bed later) was less consistent. — [Craven et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-022-01706-y); summary via [BarBend](https://barbend.com/sleep-loss-performance-study/)
- **[Moderate]** Meta-regression: performance fell ~0.4% for every additional hour awake after the sleep loss, i.e. a session later in the day after a short night is more affected than a morning session. — [Craven et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-022-01706-y); [BarBend summary](https://barbend.com/sleep-loss-performance-study/)
- **[Moderate]** A separate systematic review/meta-analysis specifically on endurance performance after sleep deprivation exists (Lopes et al. 2023, Eur J Sport Sci) — details not extracted in this session. — [Lopes et al. 2023](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1080/17461391.2022.2155583)
- **[Expert]** 2021 BJSM expert consensus on athlete sleep recommends an individualised approach that considers the athlete's perceived sleep need rather than a one-size-fits-all 7–9 h; includes sleep education, questionnaire screening, and referral for moderate–severe sleep problems. — [Walsh et al. 2021, BJSM](https://www.sportgeneeskunde.com/wp-content/uploads/Br-J-Sports-Med-2021-Walsh-consensus-statement-sleep-and-the-athlete.pdf); [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Sleep-and-the-athlete:-narrative-review-and-2021-Walsh-Halson/b145e4bfe0eb3d87eab046ab91b35814c825ba74)
- **[Moderate, population caveat]** Adolescent athletes sleeping <8 h/night had higher injury rates (Milewski 2014); a later meta-analysis reported sleep <8 h was independently associated with a ~1.7-fold increase in injury risk. Evidence is in adolescents, observational. — [Milewski et al. 2014, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25028798/); [Gao et al. 2019 meta-analysis abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2325967119S00132)

### Inferences
- App rule (short night): if logged sleep <6 h, flag the day; prefer to keep easy aerobic/hiking volume and move/downgrade high-intensity intervals or heavy strength top sets (e.g. −1 set or RPE cap ~7) rather than skip entirely. If a hard session can't be moved, do it earlier in the day (0.4%/h-awake finding). Grade: [Expert/inference from Strong data].
- Waking early to fit training in (late restriction) is the most harmful pattern — a planning rule could avoid scheduling early-morning hard sessions after late evenings (e.g. after Thursday drinks).
- App could display a target of "your usual sleep need, typically ≥7–8 h" with ≥8 h flagged as protective during the heaviest build weeks — the 8 h number is extrapolated from adolescent injury data. [Weak for adults]

### Gaps
- No adult-specific prospective data on sleep and injury risk were retrieved; the 1.7x figure is from adolescents.
- Effects of one short night on the *following* day (day +2) and on multi-day hiking were not found.
- Sleep extension / napping effect sizes (e.g. naps after short nights) not researched in this session.

## 2. Nutrition for concurrent training

### 2a. Protein (amount and distribution)

#### Takeaway
1.4–2.0 g/kg/day protein suffices for most exercising people; in a calorie deficit, higher intakes (up to 2.3–3.1 g/kg/day) help retain lean mass. Spread over meals: ~0.25 g/kg (20–40 g) per serving every 3–4 h.

#### Cited Findings
- **[Strong]** ISSN 2017: daily protein of 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day is sufficient for most exercising individuals to build and maintain muscle. — [Jäger et al. 2017, ISSN position stand (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5477153/)
- **[Moderate]** ISSN 2017: higher intakes of 2.3–3.1 g/kg/day may be needed to maximise retention of lean mass in resistance-trained people during hypocaloric periods. — [ISSN 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5477153/)
- **[Moderate]** ISSN 2017: per-serving dose ~0.25 g/kg of high-quality protein or an absolute 20–40 g, ideally evenly distributed every 3–4 h. — [ISSN 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5477153/)

#### Inferences
- For a ~75 kg athlete: 105–150 g/day baseline; 4 meals of ~25–40 g. In a deficit week, aim for the top of the range (≥2.0 g/kg, i.e. ≥150 g). MacroFactor already sets protein targets; the app can just check/show "protein ≥1.6 g/kg" as a green light.

### 2b. Carbohydrate periodisation and fueling long sessions

#### Takeaway
Match carbohydrate to the session ("fuel for the work required"): high carbohydrate before/during long or hard sessions, lower on easy/rest days. During sessions of ~1–2.5 h take 30–60 g carbohydrate/h; for longer sessions up to 90 g/h.

#### Cited Findings
- **[Strong/Expert]** ACSM/AND/DC 2016 joint position: 30–60 g carbohydrate per hour for endurance exercise of 1–2.5 h, and up to 90 g/h for longer/more intense events. — [Thomas, Erdman, Burke 2016, ACSM position (PDF)](http://drugfreesport.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Position-stand-on-Nutrition-Athletic-Performance-ACSM-2016-1.pdf)
- **[Moderate/Expert]** "Fuel for the work required": carbohydrate availability adjusted to the demands of the upcoming session(s); train-low tactics include twice-a-day training, fasted training, post-exercise CHO restriction, "sleep low, train low". Augmented cell signalling (73% of 11 studies), gene expression (75% of 12) and oxidative-enzyme increases (78% of 9) seen when sessions start within a certain glycogen range — largely mechanistic evidence, mostly in trained athletes. — [Impey et al. 2018, Sports Medicine (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5889771/)
- **[Moderate]** A 5-week periodised-carbohydrate diet did not improve maximal lactate steady state capacity vs a high-carbohydrate diet in well-trained cyclists — performance benefits of train-low are not consistently shown. — [MDPI Nutrients 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/16/2/318)

#### Inferences
- App rule: sessions/hikes >75–90 min → show "take 30–60 g CHO/h (e.g. 1 gel/banana/handful of sweets per 30–45 min)"; >2.5 h long hikes/marathon-pace long runs → "up to 60–90 g/h, practise gut tolerance". Grade: [Strong for range; Expert for exact cut-offs].
- Train-low tactics are optional and low priority for a recreational athlete who is also in a calorie deficit at times — the risk of stacking low carbohydrate on low energy availability (see 2c) outweighs uncertain gains. [Expert/inference]
- Daily carbohydrate g/kg ranges (commonly cited from ACSM 2016 as roughly 3–5 g/kg light days, 5–7 moderate, 6–10 high volume) were **not re-verified in this session** — confirm against the ACSM PDF before showing in the app.

### 2c. Training in a calorie deficit; low energy availability / REDs (IOC 2023)

#### Takeaway
Strength gains survive a deficit, lean-mass gains don't: a deficit of ~500 kcal/day blocks lean-mass gain. Low energy availability (LEA) exists on a spectrum from benign/adaptable (mild, transient) to problematic (prolonged/severe), the latter driving REDs. Males tolerate lower EA before symptoms, but long-term deficits combined with high endurance volume should trigger warning flags.

#### Cited Findings
- **[Strong]** Meta-analysis/meta-regression (59 studies, 1,495 participants): lean-mass gains with resistance training were impaired in an energy deficit (ES −0.57, p=0.02), but strength gains were comparable; a deficit of ~500 kcal/day prevented lean-mass gains. Authors advise avoiding deficits >500 kcal/day when aiming to preserve lean mass. — [Murphy & Koehler 2022, Scand J Med Sci Sports](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/sms.14075)
- **[Expert]** IOC 2023 REDs consensus: REDs is a syndrome of impaired health and performance caused by exposure to low energy availability (energy intake inadequate relative to exercise energy expenditure); EA = dietary energy left for body functions after exercise expenditure. — [Mountjoy et al. 2023, BJSM (IOC PDF)](https://stillmed.olympics.com/media/Documents/Athletes/Medical-Scientific/Consensus-Statements/REDs/BJSM-IOC-consensus-statement-on-Relative-Energy-Deficiency-in-Sport-REDs.pdf); summary via [Wu Tsai HPA](https://humanperformancealliance.org/news/2023-international-olympic-committee-consensus-statement-on-reds/)
- **[Expert]** IOC 2023 conceptual model: LEA spans "adaptable" (mild and/or transient, benign) to "problematic" (prolonged and/or severe), with REDs risk rising with duration/severity of exposure. — [Wu Tsai HPA summary of IOC 2023](https://humanperformancealliance.org/news/2023-international-olympic-committee-consensus-statement-on-reds/)
- **[Moderate/Expert]** The universal 30 kcal/kg FFM/day threshold for females is debated; for males the threshold where symptoms appear is less well understood but appears lower (~9–25 kcal/kg FFM/day). — secondary summary in search results citing IOC 2023 and related literature; [IOC REDs consensus](https://stillmed.olympics.com/media/Documents/Athletes/Medical-Scientific/Consensus-Statements/REDs/BJSM-IOC-consensus-statement-on-Relative-Energy-Deficiency-in-Sport-REDs.pdf); [IOC REDs CAT2](https://stillmed.olympics.com/media/Documents/Athletes/Medical-Scientific/Consensus-Statements/REDs/IOC-REDs-CAT-V2.pdf) (exact wording not verified — full text unavailable)
- **[Expert]** REDs CAT2 indicators include subclinically low total/free testosterone (primary indicator; clinically low counts as a severe primary indicator), and low resting metabolic rate (<30 kcal/kg FFM/day or RMR ratio <0.90). — [IOC REDs CAT2 tool](https://stillmed.olympics.com/media/Documents/Athletes/Medical-Scientific/Consensus-Statements/REDs/IOC-REDs-CAT-V2.pdf) (via search summary)
- **[Expert]** IOC 2023 recommends limiting body-composition assessment in athletes <18 because of body-dissatisfaction/disordered-eating concerns. — [PMC review "Does REDs syndrome exist?"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11561064/)

#### Inferences
- App rule: if MacroFactor shows a sustained deficit, cap it at ~500 kcal/day (≈0.5% body mass/week) and avoid it entirely in peak weeks (longest hikes/runs, taper, the trek itself). Grade: [Strong for lean mass; Expert for the performance link].
- Practical LEA warning flags a recreational male can self-check (from CAT2 indicator categories): unexplained performance decline over weeks, persistent fatigue, low libido/mood changes, frequent illness, a bone-stress injury, or reduced morning erections; ≥2 flags while in a deficit → stop the deficit and consider a doctor. Exact CAT2 checklist wording not verified. [Expert]
- On long-session days, fuel the session even in a deficit week (shift the deficit to rest days) — "within-day" deficits around training are a REDs concern in the literature, but this was not verified from the full IOC text in this session.

#### Gaps
- Exact IOC 2023 text on within-day energy deficits and on low carbohydrate availability as a REDs driver could not be retrieved.
- Daily carbohydrate g/kg targets per training load not verified in-session.
- No study found on deficit size vs endurance progression specifically in concurrent-training recreational adults.

## 3. Alcohol (1–2 drinks vs larger amounts)

### Takeaway
Large post-exercise doses (~1.5 g/kg, ≈12 drinks) reduce muscle protein synthesis by ~24–37% even with protein. Low doses (~0.5 g/kg or less) are unlikely to impair most aspects of recovery, but even low doses measurably worsen overnight heart-rate-based recovery in a dose-dependent way.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate, single RCT, high dose]** After concurrent (resistance + endurance) exercise, ~12 standard drinks (1.5 g/kg) over 8 h reduced myofibrillar protein synthesis even when co-ingested with 25 g protein: MPS rose ~109% above rest with protein alone, ~57% with alcohol + protein, ~29% with alcohol + carbohydrate. — [Parr et al. 2014, PLoS One (Bond Univ. record)](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/alcohol-ingestion-impairs-maximal-post-exercise-rates-of-myofibri/); [summary RealClearScience](https://www.realclearscience.com/journal_club/2014/02/20/if_you_booze_after_exercise_you_lose_108518.html)
- **[Expert]** The Parr study modelled binge drinking; moderate intake likely would not cause such marked reductions but this remains unstudied. — [RealClearScience summary](https://www.realclearscience.com/journal_club/2014/02/20/if_you_booze_after_exercise_you_lose_108518.html)
- **[Moderate/Expert]** Review: if alcohol is consumed after exercise, ~0.5 g/kg body weight is unlikely to impact most aspects of recovery; a low dose did not impair skeletal muscle performance after exercise-induced muscle damage. — [Barnes 2014, Sports Medicine](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-014-0192-8)
- **[Moderate, large observational]** Real-world study of Finnish employees: alcohol dose-dependently increased sympathetic and decreased parasympathetic activity during the first 3 h of sleep; HRV-derived recovery decreased by 9.3, 24.0 and 39.2 percentage units with low, moderate and high intake respectively. Effects were similar in physically active and sedentary people and stronger in younger people. — [Pietilä et al. 2018, JMIR Mental Health](https://mental.jmir.org/2018/1/e23/)

### Inferences
- Arithmetic: a 330 ml 5% beer ≈ 13 g ethanol; 1–2 beers ≈ 13–26 g ≈ 0.17–0.35 g/kg for a 75 kg person — below the 0.5 g/kg "unlikely to impair recovery" dose from Barnes 2014, but in the "low" range that still lowered overnight HRV recovery (Pietilä). [Inference]
- Practical rules for Thursday drinks: (1) do the strength session before, then eat a protein-rich meal (≥25–40 g protein) before/with the drinks; (2) cap at 1–2 drinks, alternate with water; (3) don't schedule intervals or the key long run/hike for early Friday morning (combines alcohol + likely shorter sleep/late restriction); Friday as easy or rest day fits well; (4) expect wearable HRV/RHR to look worse Friday — the app should not treat that single dip as an overreaching signal. Grade: [Expert/inference].
- 3+ drinks, especially within the post-exercise window, moves toward the doses shown to impair MPS. [Weak extrapolation]

### Gaps
- No RCT on 1–2 drinks and next-day running/hiking performance or MPS was found.
- Pietilä's exact low/moderate/high g/kg cut-offs were not verified in-session.
- Alcohol's effect on sleep architecture (REM suppression) meta-analysis evidence not retrieved in-session.

## 4. Illness: neck check, fever, return to training

### Takeaway
The "neck check" (above-neck symptoms → light training allowed; below-neck symptoms or fever → rest) is widely used but has no direct scientific validation; it is reasonable expert practice. Return should be gradual and symptom-guided.

### Cited Findings
- **[Expert]** Neck check: athletes with above-the-neck symptoms (sore throat, runny/blocked nose) may resume exercise at low intensity and short duration and continue if tolerated; with below-the-neck symptoms (fever, myalgia, cough) rest until symptoms resolve. — [Return to Play After Infectious Disease (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7123245/); [Univ. Pretoria SEMLI guidance](https://www.up.ac.za/Sport-Exercise-Medicine-and-Lifestyle-Institute/news/medical-guidance-athletes-returning-sport-after-acute-respiratory-illness)
- **[Expert]** The neck check has no scientific evidence base and its validity as a return-to-sport guide has been challenged ("nonscientific but may be partly useful"). — [Sport and exercise during viral acute respiratory illness — Time to revisit, J Sport Health Sci 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2095254623001187) (abstract via search; full text 403)
- **[Moderate]** Systematic review/meta-analysis (IOC consensus work): data on days to return to sport after acute respiratory illness are very limited; ~20% of acute respiratory illnesses cause >1 day time loss and symptoms last ~7 days on average. — [IOC consensus on acute respiratory illness in athletes, part 1](https://stillmed.olympics.com/media/Documents/Athletes/Medical-Scientific/Consensus-Statements/Acute-respiratory-illness-in-athletes-part-1.pdf) (via search summary; PDF returned 503)
- **[Expert, coaching practice]** Be symptom-free for 24–48 h depending on severity; begin with short, easy aerobic sessions over the first 2–3 days; allow 1–2 days of build-up per day missed to return to pre-illness load. — [Triathlete, ER physician guidance](https://www.triathlete.com/training/injury-prevention/exercise-after-cold-flu-strep-covid/)

### Inferences
- App decision tree (Expert):
  1. Fever (≥38 °C), body aches, chest symptoms, GI illness → no training; mark sessions as "skipped – illness" (append-only log, no reschedule cascade).
  2. Above-neck only, no fever → optional easy session ≤30–45 min at low intensity; stop if worse; no intervals/heavy lifting.
  3. Return: after 24–48 h symptom-free (fever-free without medication), day 1–3 easy/short, then rebuild over roughly as many days as were missed (1–2 days per day missed).
  4. Red flags → doctor: chest pain, palpitations, disproportionate breathlessness, or symptoms persisting >7–10 days. (Myocarditis concern after viral illness/fever is the standard rationale; not verified in-session.)

### Gaps
- Full IOC 2022/23 acute respiratory illness consensus (Schwellnus et al.) text — including any formal graded return-to-sport protocol — could not be retrieved.
- No RCT evidence on training through mild URTI and outcomes.

## 5. Missed training: detraining, resuming, catching up

### Takeaway
Fitness is lost slowly: <2 weeks off costs little; over ~4 weeks of complete rest trained athletes lose roughly 4–20% VO2max and ~7–10% max strength, while recently-acquired gains are lost faster. Do not "catch up" missed sessions; resume at reduced load and continue the plan.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate]** Mujika & Padilla (2000/2001) define short-term detraining as <4 weeks and long-term as >4 weeks of insufficient stimulus; these remain the foundational reviews. — [Mujika & Padilla, Sports Med (Semantic Scholar)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Detraining:-Loss-of-Training-Induced-Physiological-Mujika-Padilla/976e67d8710929b988ba84e15d4b1c10e4b09420)
- **[Moderate]** Highly trained endurance athletes lose ~4–14% VO2max in short-term detraining (and 6–20% over ~4 weeks of complete cessation); recently trained individuals lose less in absolute terms (3–6%); over long-term detraining, recently acquired VO2max gains are completely lost. — [SimpliFaster summary of Mujika & Padilla](https://simplifaster.com/articles/detraining-the-loss-of-training-induced-adaptations-in-the-short-term/); [Mujika & Padilla](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Detraining:-Loss-of-Training-Induced-Physiological-Mujika-Padilla/976e67d8710929b988ba84e15d4b1c10e4b09420) (secondary summaries; exact numbers not checked against original)
- **[Moderate]** Strength is largely maintained for up to ~4 weeks of inactivity; trained athletes lose roughly 7–10% max strength over 4 weeks of cessation. — [getomn.io summary](https://getomn.io/blog/posts/detraining-how-fast-you-actually-lose-strength/); [sci-fit summary](https://sci-fit.net/detraining/) (aggregators — weaker sourcing)
- **[Expert, coaching practice]** 2–5 days missed: fitness not meaningfully changed; 6–14 days missed: step back within the progression by 1–2 weeks (repeat the load from 1–2 weeks before the break). Coaches should not have athletes make up missed workouts; near an event, key sessions may be rescheduled into the next 2 weeks. — [Triathlete, ER physician/coach guidance](https://www.triathlete.com/training/injury-prevention/exercise-after-cold-flu-strep-covid/)
- **[Expert]** After returning, downgrade planned intervals by one intensity zone for the first sessions. — [Triathlete](https://www.triathlete.com/training/injury-prevention/exercise-after-cold-flu-strep-covid/)

### Inferences
- App resume rules (Expert, derived from the above):
  - ≤5 days missed: continue the plan as written; first session easy.
  - 6–14 days: repeat the previous week's (or the week-before's) load; first week back at ~70–80% of pre-break volume, no maximal efforts; strength at ~1–2 fewer sets/lighter top sets. (Percentages are coaching heuristics, not trial-derived.)
  - 2–4 weeks: rebuild over ~2 weeks starting ~50–60% of previous volume; re-check the GR5 milestone ladder timing.
  - >4 weeks: treat as a new base phase; recompute the 16-week plan rather than forcing the original dates.
- "Catch up" is consistently discouraged: cramming missed volume raises load spikes; the scheduler should drop or swap missed sessions, keeping only a key long hike/long run if it fits without violating the 48 h leg-heavy rule. This matches ASCEND's existing cascade/append-only design.

### Gaps
- No RCT data on the optimal percentage of load to resume at after 1 vs 2 weeks; numbers above are expert heuristics.
- Primary detraining numbers taken from secondary summaries; original Mujika & Padilla tables not checked.
- Acute:chronic workload injury-risk evidence (relevant to "no catch-up") not researched in-session.

## 6. Caffeine and other legal ergogenic aids for long hikes/runs

### Takeaway
Caffeine at 3–6 mg/kg ~60 min before exercise is the best-supported legal aid, with the most consistent moderate-to-large benefits in aerobic endurance; doses as low as ~2 mg/kg may work, and ~9 mg/kg adds side effects without extra benefit.

### Cited Findings
- **[Strong]** Caffeine consistently improves exercise performance at 3–6 mg/kg; minimal effective dose may be as low as ~2 mg/kg; very high doses (~9 mg/kg) cause frequent side effects and are not required. — [Guest et al. 2021, ISSN caffeine position stand (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7777221/)
- **[Strong]** Aerobic endurance shows the most consistent moderate-to-large benefits from caffeine, with marked inter-individual variation. — [ISSN 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7777221/)
- **[Moderate]** Most common timing is 60 min pre-exercise; caffeinated gum, gels and chews also improve (mainly aerobic) performance, useful mid-session. — [ISSN 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7777221/)

### Inferences
- For a 75 kg athlete: ~150–225 mg (≈1.5–2 strong coffees) 60 min before a long run; on a long hike, a caffeinated gel (often ~50–100 mg) in the afternoon. Avoid caffeine late in the day to protect sleep (ties to section 1). [Inference]
- On multi-day treks, high daily caffeine can disrupt sleep in huts; moderate doses morning/midday are a sensible default. [Expert/inference]

### Gaps
- Other aids (dietary nitrate/beetroot, sodium bicarbonate, beta-alanine, electrolytes/sodium for long hikes) were not researched in this session; IOC 2018 supplement consensus not retrieved.
- Caffeine-sleep interaction data (hours before bed) not retrieved in-session.
