# Event-specific preparation for the GR5 (Alps + northern sections), recreational adult, ±12 kg pack, ±20 km / ±1,000 m D+ and D− per day

Evidence grading used throughout:
- **Strong**: guideline with high-grade recommendation, or systematic review / meta-analysis with consistent results
- **Moderate**: RCTs or consistent controlled lab studies, but small or indirect populations (soldiers, athletes, young men)
- **Weak**: single observational study, cross-sectional data, extrapolation
- **Expert opinion**: guide companies, hiking federations, consensus without trial data, or my own inference

Search-process caveat: several primary papers (Hawke & Jensen 2020 trekking-pole review, Worthing 2017 blister review, the full WMS 2024 heat guideline) returned HTTP 403 or sat behind a paywall. For those, the numbers below come from abstracts or search snippets, and I say so where it matters.

## 1. Route profile and altitude: is altitude relevant on the GR5?

### Takeaway
The GR5 Alps tops out at about 2,760–2,800 m. Sleeping altitudes are lower (villages and huts, mostly below about 2,500 m; see Gaps). By WMS 2024 criteria this makes the route **low risk for acute mountain sickness (AMS)**. Altitude still costs roughly **6% of VO2max per 1,000 m**, so high stages feel harder. No formal acclimatisation is needed. Start with the lower northern/Chablais stages, and if possible avoid flying in and sleeping at about 2,500 m+ on night 1.

### Cited Findings
- **Highest points depend on the line walked.** On the official GTA 36-stage itinerary the high point is the **Col de la Leisse (Vanoise), 2,758 m (IGN) / 2,761 m (other sources)**. The **GR55 variant** crosses the **Col de Chavière at 2,796 m**. The Val-d'Isère → Bonneval line crosses the **Col de l'Iseran at 2,764 m** — [Ultimate France: Hiking the GR5](https://www.ultimatefrance.com/hiking/french-alps/gr5); [Wikipedia: Col de l'Iseran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Col_de_l%27Iseran)
- NKBV describes the GR5 Alps as two week-long blocks: Lac Léman → Les Houches, then Les Houches → Vanoise → Modane. The second block has **Col de Chavière at about 2,800 m as its highest point**. Earlier passes: **Col d'Anterne 2,257 m** and **Col du Brévent 2,367 m**. NKBV mentions "long climbs" and "serious descents", and one section with cables/brackets where it suggests a via-ferrata set for experienced hikers — [NKBV: De GR5 dwars door de Franse Alpen](https://nkbv.nl/actueel/blog/de-gr5-dwars-door-de-franse-alpen.html) (**expert opinion**)
- The full GR5 runs Hoek van Holland → Nice, about **2,150 km in about 93 stages**. It crosses the Ardennes, Vosges and Jura, which are lower and gentler, before the Alps. In France it splits into the Grande Traversée des Vosges, du Jura and des Alpes — [Wikipedia NL: Wandelroute GR5](https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wandelroute_GR5); [Hike5: De GR5 in 93 etappes](https://hike5.blog/gr5/)
- **WMS 2024 altitude guideline:** unacclimatised people are at risk of altitude illness **above 2,500 m**, and susceptible people can get AMS **as low as 2,000 m**. **Sleeping altitude matters more than the highest altitude reached during the day.** Controlling the ascent rate is "highly effective". **Low risk** = no history of altitude illness and ascending to **≤2,800 m**, or taking ≥2 days to reach 2,500–3,000 m and then raising sleeping altitude by <500 m/day. **Moderate risk** = prior AMS and reaching 2,500–2,800 m in 1 day, or ascending >500 m/day (above 3,000 m) — [Luks et al., WMS Acute Altitude Illness 2024 Update, Wilderness Environ Med](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1016/j.wem.2023.05.013); [ResearchGate copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/374636575_Wilderness_Medical_Society_Clinical_Practice_Guidelines_for_the_Prevention_Diagnosis_and_Treatment_of_Acute_Altitude_Illness_2024_Update) (**strong**, guideline)
- **Performance loss:** in endurance athletes, **VO2max fell linearly by 6.3% per 1,000 m** (individual range 4.6–7.5%) between 300 and 2,800 m. Time-to-exhaustion at a fixed supramaximal speed fell **14.5% per 1,000 m** — [Wehrlin & Hallén 2006, Eur J Appl Physiol (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7456729_Linear_decrease_in_VO2max_and_performance_with_increasing_altitude_in_endurance_athletes) (**moderate**; trained athletes, acute exposure). A training resource gives a broader **6–10% loss per 1,000 m** of acute exposure — [Uphill Athlete: Endurance Training at Altitude (PDF)](https://www.uphillathlete.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/Endurance-Training-at-Altitude.pdf) (**expert opinion**)

### Inferences
- At 2,500–2,800 m a hiker loses roughly **10–15% of aerobic capacity** compared with lowland training (6.3% × about 1.5–2.3 km above 300 m). The same climb rate will feel 1–2 RPE points harder. Advice: slow the uphill pace on the high Vanoise/Queyras days rather than trying to hold lowland pace. (Inference from Wehrlin & Hallén; **weak**/extrapolated.)
- **AMS risk:** the GR5 never goes above about 2,800 m, and most nights are in valleys or huts below about 2,500 m. Starting at Lac Léman and walking south means a naturally gradual ascent profile. The trek fits the WMS "low risk" category, and **no acetazolamide or formal acclimatisation protocol is indicated** for someone with no history of altitude illness. Mild headache or poor sleep on the first nights above about 2,000 m is possible. Practical rule: descend if headache plus nausea/fatigue does not settle with rest. (Inference from WMS 2024; **strong** underlying guideline, applied by inference.)
- Altitude does not need its own training block. The training implication is **aerobic headroom**: be able to hike 1,000 m D+ at a comfortably sub-threshold pace at lowland altitude.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a verified list of the highest **sleeping** altitudes on the GR5. Huts in the Vanoise such as Refuge de la Leisse, Col de la Vanoise and Péclet-Polset are believed to sit about 2,400–2,500 m, but I have not confirmed this, so treat it as unverified. The official GTA stage list (grande-traversee-alpes.com) did not appear in search results.
- No GR5-specific AMS incidence data exists that I could find.

## 2. Heat, weather and hydration

### Takeaway
Summer valley stages (Maurienne, Ubaye, the descent to Nice) can be hot. **About 7–14 days of 60–120 min daily exercise in the heat** produces most heat acclimatisation, and even **3 × 90 min in a week cuts heat strain about 20%** (strong/moderate). Fluid targets: drink to limit loss to **<2% of body mass**. Typical sweat rates are **0.5–1.5 L/h**, wider in heat. Add **sodium of about 460–690 mg per litre** of fluid on hot or long days.

### Cited Findings
- **WMS 2024 heat illness guideline (as summarised by AAFP 2025):** "**Three 90-minute sessions** of outdoor exercise in 1 week can reduce subsequent heat-related physiologic strain by approximately **20%**", and "about **80% of acclimatization** can be gained in **7 days** of moderate exertion in heat for **1 to 2 hours per day**" — [AAFP 2025 summary of WMS Heat Illness Guidelines](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2025/1200/practice-guidelines-heat-illness.html); original: [Eifling et al. 2024, Wilderness Environ Med](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10806032241227924) (**strong**, guideline; exact evidence grades were behind a paywall)
- The WMS 2024 guideline (per search snippet) says **1–2 h/day of heat-exposed exertion for 10–14 days** gives substantial acclimatisation, and **at least 7 days** is recommended for full acclimatisation — [WMS Heat Illness 2024 (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/378639078_Wilderness_Medical_Society_Clinical_Practice_Guidelines_for_the_Prevention_and_Treatment_of_Heat_Illness_2024_Update) (**strong**)
- **Périard et al. 2015 consensus (Scand J Med Sci Sports):** at minimum **7 consecutive days**, 1–2 h of sustained aerobic exercise in heat. Start **at least 2 weeks before** the event. Once acclimatised, the state can be kept with heat sessions **every 2nd–3rd day** — [Frontiers in Physiology 2018 (citing Périard 2015)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.01851/full); [GSSI SSE #153](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/sse-153-heat-acclimatization-to-improve-athletic-performance-in-warm-hot-environments) (**strong/moderate**)
- **Sweat rate rises after 10–14 days of heat exposure**, so fluid needs go *up* after acclimatisation — [NATA Position Statement: Fluid Replacement for the Physically Active (2017)](https://www.nata.org/sites/default/files/2025-08/fluid_replacement_for_the_physically_active.pdf) (**strong**)
- **Sweat rates in adults range from 0.5 to 4.0 L/h.** Endurance athletes typically average 1.0–1.5 L/h. Dehydration of **≥2% body mass** impairs thermoregulation, raises cardiovascular strain and often impairs aerobic performance — [NATA 2017](https://www.nata.org/sites/default/files/2025-08/fluid_replacement_for_the_physically_active.pdf); [ACSM Position Stand: Exercise and Fluid Replacement](https://www.khsaa.org/sportsmedicine/heat/exerciseandfluidreplacement.pdf) (**strong**)
- **Sodium:** ACSM-aligned guidance is **20–30 mEq Na/L (about 460–690 mg/L)** in fluids during prolonged exercise. Sweat sodium averages about **0.8 g per litre of sweat** (varies with genetics, diet, acclimatisation) — [ACSM fluid replacement summary](https://www.racewhitetail.org/resources/hydration-new-fluid-replacement-recommendations-acsm); [NATA position statement](https://www.nata.org/sites/default/files/2025-08/FluidReplacementsForAthletes.pdf) (**moderate**)
- **Season:** the classic GR5 Alps season is **June–September**. High cols can still hold **snow until the end of June**, so ask hut wardens or the PGHM about conditions. Staffed refuges are generally open **mid-June to mid-September**, and booking is strongly recommended in July–August — [Échappées Montagnardes FAQ GR5](https://www.echappeesmontagnardes.fr/gr5-grande-traversee-des-vosges-du-jura-et-des-alpes/faq-gr5-tout-savoir-pour-reussir-la-traversee-des-vosges-du-jura-et-des-alpes/); [Mollow: GR5 Alpes itinéraire](https://www.mollow.eu/article/gr5-alpes-itineraire-etapes-conseils) (**expert opinion**)

### Inferences
- **Pre-trip heat protocol (practical):** in the **last 2–3 weeks before departure**, do **5–10 sessions of 60–90 min** of hiking/cycling in warm conditions or overdressed indoors, ideally on consecutive days. Finish **within about 1 week of departure** so the adaptation persists, then keep 2–3 warm sessions per week until leaving. A Dutch/Belgian summer is often not hot enough on its own, so overdressing, a sauna after training, or indoor sessions can substitute. Sauna and passive-heat protocols were not reviewed here, so that part is weaker evidence. (Inference from WMS/Périard; **moderate**.)
- **On-trail hydration target for this hiker:** about **0.4–0.8 L/h** in temperate mountain conditions, rising to **0.8–1.2 L/h** on hot valley climbs. That means carrying about 1.5–2.5 L between water points, plus electrolyte tabs or salty food on hot days. Weigh yourself before and after a hot training hike to estimate personal sweat rate: 1 kg lost ≈ 1 L. (Inference from NATA/ACSM ranges; **moderate**.)
- Don't over-drink: drinking well beyond sweat losses with no sodium raises hyponatraemia risk. This is consistent with NATA/ACSM "drink to limit loss to <2%" rather than "drink as much as possible".

### Gaps
- No GR5-specific temperature or thunderstorm climatology was retrieved. Afternoon convective storms in the Alps in July–August are widely reported by guides, but I did not get a citable source. The usual advice is to start early and be over high cols by early afternoon; treat it as **expert opinion, unsourced here**.
- The WMS 2024 heat guideline's exact per-recommendation grades (1A/1B/1C) could not be read (paywall).

## 3. Fueling on multi-day stages and recovery between days

### Takeaway
Expect roughly **3,500–4,500+ kcal/day** on full mountain stages with a 12 kg pack. Field studies of comparable multi-day efforts measured **about 4,500–4,900 kcal/day** with doubly labelled water (DLW), though in harsher settings. Most trekkers run an energy deficit. Practical targets: **30–60 g carbohydrate per hour** while moving, **1.2–2.0 g/kg/day protein**, and deliberate snacking, because hut dinners alone usually don't close the gap.

### Cited Findings
- **DLW-measured total daily energy expenditure in multi-day field efforts:**
  - Women on a 7-day ski trek to the North Pole (sled-hauling): **18.67 ± 1.72 MJ/d ≈ 4,460 kcal/d** — [POWER study, PubMed 40588629](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40588629/); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12208795/) (**moderate**; colder and heavier than GR5)
  - High-altitude exercise/expedition: **19.07 ± 2.37 MJ/d ≈ 4,558 ± 566 kcal/d** — [Am J Physiol 1994, DLW at high altitude](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/abs/10.1152/ajpregu.1994.266.3.R966) (**moderate**)
  - US Marines, 11-day cold-weather field exercise at 2,200–2,550 m: **4,919 ± 190 kcal/d** — reported in the same high-altitude DLW literature [Am J Physiol 1994](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/abs/10.1152/ajpregu.1994.266.3.R966) (**moderate**; this attribution comes from the search summary and may belong to a companion DLW paper; verify)
- **Footwear mass adds energy cost:** about **0.7–1.0% per 100 g** added per pair of footwear — [Jones et al. 1986, Ergonomics](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00140138608968277) (**moderate**; relevant to fueling and to the boots-vs-shoes choice)
- **Carbohydrate during exercise:** about **30–60 g/h** to maintain blood glucose — [ACSM / Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics / Dietitians of Canada Joint Position 2016 (PDF)](https://sky.sausport.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/American-College-of-Sports-Medicine_Joint-Position_Nutrition_and_Athletic_Performance_2016.pdf) (**strong** for athletes; indirect for hiking)
- **Protein:** the search snippet gave **1.2–1.7 g/kg/day** for endurance and strength athletes. The 2016 joint position text itself states **1.2–2.0 g/kg/day**; 1.2–1.7 is the older 2009 range, so the snippet appears to mix versions — [ACSM Joint Position 2016](https://sky.sausport.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/American-College-of-Sports-Medicine_Joint-Position_Nutrition_and_Athletic_Performance_2016.pdf); [mysportscience summary](https://www.mysportscience.com/post/2016/04/02/new-position-statement-on-nutrition-and-athletic-performance) (**strong** for athletes)
- **Blog/commercial estimates** (flagged, low quality): "3,500–5,500+ kcal/day" for strenuous multi-day backpacking, and "0.5–0.7 kcal/kg/km on the flat". One blog cited a "2024 University of Innsbruck study, 4,870 kcal on a 1,200 m ascent day" that **I could not verify; do not use** — [HikeLoad blog](https://hikeload.com/blog/how-many-calories-hiking-full-day); [Packstack calculator](https://www.packstack.io/tools/backpacking-calorie-calculator) (**expert opinion / unreliable**)

### Inferences
- **Energy estimate for this hiker** (about 75–80 kg body mass + 12 kg pack, 20 km, ±1,000 m up and down, 6–8 h moving): roughly **3,500–4,500 kcal/day**. That is somewhat below the DLW figures above, which involved cold, sled-hauling or military loads. (Inference; **weak**: extrapolated, no GR5-specific measurement found.)
- **Practical eating pattern:**
  - big hut breakfast
  - eat **every 45–60 min** while moving, about 30–60 g carbohydrate/h: bread, cheese, dried fruit, nut bars, dried sausage; hikers at moderate intensity burn proportionally more fat, so the lower end (30–40 g/h) is usually enough
  - a **20–40 g protein + carbohydrate snack within about 1–2 h** of arriving
  - full demi-pension dinner
  This aligns with ACSM recovery principles. The specific timing advice is **moderate/expert opinion**.
- A daily deficit of about 500–1,000 kcal over 2–3 weeks is tolerable for most adults (about 1–2 kg mass loss). Large deficits impair recovery on back-to-back days, so plan resupply. On the GR5, villages and huts make daily resupply feasible on most stages (**expert opinion**).
- **Pre-trip practice:** do at least 2–3 long training hikes (5–7 h) eating exactly the planned trail food, to test gut tolerance (**expert opinion**).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed DLW study of recreational hut-to-hut trekkers in the Alps was found.
- No hiking-specific carbohydrate-per-hour trial was found; the 30–60 g/h figure is extrapolated from athlete guidance.

## 4. Feet: blisters, footwear, toenails and descents

### Takeaway
Blisters are the most common trek-ending nuisance: **about 62% of Camino walkers** in one study, and **wet socks at stage end** was the main modifiable risk factor. Evidence for most prevention products is **weak**. **Paper tape on known hot spots** has the best (moderate) support. Boots vs trail runners has **no good comparative injury evidence**, but every 100 g of footwear costs about 1% extra energy.

### Cited Findings
- **Worthing et al. 2017 systematic review** (Wilderness Environ Med) screened 806 articles and included **11 studies**: 5 sock, 3 antiperspirant, 3 barrier strategies. Only **2 studies had moderate confidence**, and heterogeneity prevented meta-analysis. It concluded there is "a **paucity of high-quality evidence** in support of socks, antiperspirants, or barriers". **Paper tape** had moderate-confidence evidence as an effective barrier — [Worthing, Percy & Joslin 2017, WEM](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1016/j.wem.2017.03.007); [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1080603217300923) (**moderate** for paper tape; **weak/insufficient** for socks, antiperspirants and lubricants). Source: abstract and snippets only, full text 403.
- **Camino de Santiago case-control study:** 86 hikers, mean 20 days and 456 km walked. **61.6% developed blisters**, most often on the 2nd and 5th toes, first metatarsal head and lateral heel. **Wet socks at stage end:** 73.6% of blister cases vs 45.5% of controls (p = 0.009). Higher foot-skin hydration predicted blisters; women and younger walkers had higher hydration. **Footwear type, waterproofing, new vs used footwear and sock changes during the day were *not* significantly associated** — [Influence of skin hydration level on blisters during hiking, PMC11646658](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11646658/) (**weak**, observational)
- Another hiking study reportedly found **km walked on asphalt** and **wet socks at day end** were risk factors, while walking on dirt/grass/gravel and **custom plantar orthoses** were protective — [Prevalence and risk factors of foot dermal lesions during hiking, ScienceDirect 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0965206X20300723) (**weak**; from search abstract snippet, full text not read). A related RCT on sock composition exists — [Influence of sock composition on foot blisters in hikers, 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0965206X22000213); [ClinicalTrials NCT03914274](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03914274). I could not read its results, so they are a gap.
- **Footwear mass:** **+0.7–1.0% energy cost per 100 g** of extra footwear mass — [Jones et al. 1986, Ergonomics](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00140138608968277) (**moderate**)
- A 2026 scoping review on footwear in trail running and hiking exists — [Jackson et al. 2026, J Foot Ankle Res, PMC13452467](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13452467). From the search snippet it mostly covers shoe properties (foam, plates, minimalist shoes) and **does not establish** that boots reduce ankle sprains compared with trail shoes (**weak / insufficient**).
- **Pack load and plantar pressure:** in long-distance hikers, plantar pressure rises progressively once the pack exceeds about **10% of body weight**, especially at the heel. At **20% BW**, total static plantar pressure is **almost 10% higher** than with no pack — [Plantar pressure responses to backpack load in long-distance hikers, PMC12821408](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12821408/) (**weak**, cross-sectional)

### Inferences
- **Practical blister protocol (best available evidence plus expert consensus):**
  1. Keep feet dry. Use synthetic or merino socks (not cotton), change into a dry pair at lunch on wet or hot days, and air feet at breaks. The wet-sock association is the strongest modifiable signal.
  2. **Pre-tape known hot spots with paper/micropore tape** from day 1. This is the only intervention with moderate evidence.
  3. Stop and treat at the first hot spot rather than at a blister.
  4. Break in footwear on at least **4–6 long training hikes** totalling about **100+ km and including ≥1,000 m descents** with the full pack. The Camino study found no "new vs used" effect, so the main goal is to discover *your* hot spots and fit issues, not to soften the shoe.
  5. Lubricants and antiperspirants have no good evidence for or against. Try them in training, not first on the trail.
- **Boots vs trail runners for a ±12 kg pack on GR5 terrain** (rocky Alpine paths, some scree, possible late snow): no high-quality evidence picks a winner, so this is **expert opinion**.
  - Light boots or mid-cut hiking shoes add about 300–600 g per pair, so about 3–6% more energy cost by the Jones data.
  - In return they give more underfoot protection and some protection from ankle twists.
  - Trail runners suit people with strong ankles, a training history in them, and packs at the lower end.
  - Since the pack is ±12 kg and stages are ±1,000 m of descent, a **light mid-cut boot or a stiff-soled trail shoe** is a reasonable default.
  - The deciding factor should be **whatever has been trained in for ≥6 weeks**.
- **Toenails and descents:** black toenails come from the toes hitting the toe box on long descents (**expert opinion**; not researched in this pass). Practical measures:
  - trim nails short and straight
  - choose a shoe about a thumb's width (≈1 cm) longer than the foot
  - use a heel-lock lacing technique to stop the foot sliding forward
  - tighten the forefoot lacing before long descents
- Feet swell over multi-day trekking and in heat, so fit shoes in the afternoon or after a long walk (**expert opinion**).

### Gaps
- No RCT or cohort comparing ankle sprain or blister rates between hiking boots and trail runners in trekkers was found.
- Results of the 2022 sock-composition trial in hikers could not be read.
- No peer-reviewed data on subungual haematoma (black toenail) prevention was retrieved.

## 5. Trekking poles: knee load, effort, technique, when to start

### Takeaway
Poles reduce knee joint loading on descents by about **10–25%** (moderate evidence from lab biomechanics, including with a load carried). They are especially useful with a 12 kg pack and 1,000 m of daily descent. Practise with them for **at least 4–6 weeks** before the trek so the technique is automatic (expert opinion).

### Cited Findings
- **Schwameder et al. (1999, J Sports Sci):** walking downhill at a **25° decline**, poles reduced knee joint loading by **12–25%**. Ground reaction force fell about **14%** and knee flexion moment about **16%** on average. The mechanism was the force transferred to the poles plus a more forward-leaning trunk, which shortens the knee moment arm — [Knee joint forces during downhill walking with hiking poles (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/12692643_Knee_joint_forces_during_downhill_walking_with_hiking_poles); [Comparison of knee joint forces with/without poles (Academia)](https://www.academia.edu/28761054/Comparison_of_knee_joint_forces_during_downhill_walking_with_and_without_hiking_poles) (**moderate**; small lab samples)
- **Bohne & Abendroth-Smith (2007, Med Sci Sports Exerc):** downhill hiking **while carrying an external load (pack)**. Pole use significantly reduced sagittal-plane joint moments at each lower-limb joint and reduced vertical ground reaction forces — [PubMed 17218900](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17218900/) (**moderate**; directly relevant to loaded descent)
- **Hawke & Jensen 2020 review:** "Are Trekking Poles Helping or Hindering Your Hiking Experience?" in Wilderness Environ Med. It covers effort (oxygen cost, heart rate, RPE), muscle damage and balance. The full text was **not accessible (403)**, so its specific numbers are not reported here — [WEM 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1016/j.wem.2020.06.009)
- A 2026 study looked at gait adaptation in pole-assisted hiking with fatigue and step height. It is mentioned for completeness and was not read in full — [PMC12876130](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12876130/)

### Inferences
- With 1,000 m of descent per day for many days running, a 12–25% cut in knee load per step adds up to a large reduction in cumulative knee stress. Poles are **recommended** for this profile. (Inference; **moderate** underlying biomechanics.)
- **Effort:** the wider literature, recalled from training knowledge and not verified in this session, suggests poles slightly raise oxygen cost and heart rate on flat or uphill ground, because the arms do work, while RPE stays similar or lower. Treat this as **unverified**; Hawke & Jensen 2020 is the source to check.
- **Technique (expert opinion):**
  - on flats, set pole length so the elbow is at about 90°
  - shorten 5–10 cm for climbs and lengthen 5–10 cm for descents
  - plant the poles *ahead* of you on descents so they take load before the foot lands
  - use the straps correctly (hand up through the strap from below) so weight transfers through the strap rather than a tight grip
- **When to start:** use poles on **every hilly training hike for the final 6–8 weeks**, including loaded descents. Arm and shoulder muscles adapt, technique becomes automatic, and any strap chafing or grip blisters show up before the trip. (**Expert opinion**; no trial on familiarisation period found.)

### Gaps
- No verified numbers on the oxygen cost or heart rate effect of poles while carrying a 10–15 kg pack.
- No trial data on how long pole familiarisation needs to be.

## 6. Back-to-back day tolerance: recovery between stages

### Takeaway
The biggest controllable factor is **descent-induced muscle damage**. Earlier exposure to downhill walking (the "repeated bout effect") cuts post-descent soreness by **about 47–66%** (moderate evidence). The pre-trip plan should therefore deliberately include loaded descents. During the trek, recovery rests on daily energy and protein intake, sleep, and pacing the first days conservatively.

### Cited Findings
- **Maeo et al. 2017 (PLOS ONE) RCT:** 36 untrained young men, three groups of 12. A **5-minute downhill walk** (−28% slope, 5 km/h, pack = 10% of body mass) done **1 week before** a 40-minute damaging downhill walk **reduced damage markers by 47–64%** compared with control:
  - strength loss −9.9% vs −19.2%
  - peak creatine kinase (CK, a muscle-damage marker in blood) 339 vs 636 U/L
  - peak soreness 28 vs 81 mm
  Level walking as pre-conditioning gave **no protection** — [PLOS ONE 2017: Prevention of downhill walking-induced muscle damage by non-damaging downhill walking](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0173909) (**moderate**)
- Mountain trekking and downhill walking cause muscle damage that **takes several days to recover**. The protection is large when similar exercise is repeated **within several weeks** — [PLOS ONE 2017](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0173909) (**moderate**)
- A systematic review and meta-analysis found that pre-conditioning with isometric, eccentric or downhill exercise reduces the signs and symptoms of exercise-induced muscle damage — [Effects of pre-conditioning on EIMD: systematic review & meta-analysis, PMC10356650](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10356650/) (**strong/moderate**)
- Earlier exposure to downhill running is described as the most effective strategy to limit muscle damage, via the repeated bout effect — [Downhill running narrative review, PMC7674385](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7674385/) (**moderate**)

### Inferences
- **Pre-trip:** include **weekly loaded descents** (stairs, dunes, hills, or treadmill decline if available) in the last 8–10 weeks. Do at least one or two **back-to-back weekends** (2–3 consecutive days of 5–7 h with full pack). The last big descent session should be **≤2–3 weeks** before departure so the protection carries into the trek. (Inference from Maeo 2017 and RBE literature; **moderate**.)
- **On trek:**
  - make **days 1–3 the shortest/easiest**; the northern Chablais stages and the gentler northern GR5 sections fit this naturally
  - eat protein and carbohydrate soon after arriving
  - aim for 7–9 h of sleep; hut dormitories make earplugs and an eye mask worthwhile
  - do 10–15 min of easy walking or mobility in the evening rather than total rest
  - plan a **rest or short day every 5–7 stages**
  (**Expert opinion**, consistent with general sports-recovery principles; no trekking-specific trial found.)
- Soreness that peaks on day 2–3 and then fades is expected muscle damage. Pain that worsens day after day in one location (shin, Achilles, knee, foot) is a warning sign for an overuse injury and needs a shorter day or rest (**expert opinion**).

### Gaps
- No trials on recovery interventions (compression, cold water, massage, NSAIDs) specifically in multi-day trekkers were retrieved.
- No data on sleep quality in mountain huts and how it affects next-day performance.

## 7. Pack fit, weight targets and training with the actual pack

### Takeaway
For a ±12 kg pack, the common hiking guideline of **≤20% of body weight** (≤15% ideal) is met by anyone above about 60 kg. Biomechanical data suggest strain on the feet rises from about **10% BW** upward. This rule of thumb rests on **weak evidence and military tradition**, not trials in recreational hikers. Train with the **actual packed pack** for the final 6–8 weeks.

### Cited Findings
- In long-distance hikers, plantar pressure rises progressively above about **10% BW**. At **20% BW**, total static plantar pressure was almost **10% higher** than without a pack. The authors suggest limiting the pack to about 10% BW to reduce plantar stress — [PMC12821408](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12821408/) (**weak**, cross-sectional)
- The "20% rule" traces back to mid-20th-century **military load-carriage research**. Soldiers could carry about **30% BW** before performance dropped sharply, with comfort and endurance best around **15–20%**. Hiking media recommend **≤20% max, 15% ideal** for multi-day trips — [Advnture: the 20% myth](https://www.advnture.com/features/how-much-should-backpack-weigh) (**expert opinion**; secondary/journalistic)
- A study of recreational female hikers looked at prolonged load carriage at different load masses. It reports load-dependent changes in posture, heart rate and perceived exertion; specific thresholds were not read — [PubMed 20870217](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20870217/) (**moderate**; full results not retrieved)

### Inferences
- **Target:** 12 kg is 15% BW at 80 kg, 17% at 70 kg and 20% at 60 kg. For a hut-to-hut GR5 with no tent or stove, 12 kg *including* water and food is achievable. A base weight of about **7–9 kg** plus 1.5–2.5 L water plus 1 day of food is a reasonable target (**expert opinion**).
- **Pack fitting:**
  - the hip belt sits on the iliac crest and carries about 70–80% of the load
  - shoulder straps wrap the shoulders without gaps
  - load lifters at about 45°
  - the torso length matches the back length
  - heavy items close to the spine and between the shoulder blades
  These are the common outfitter and guide recommendations (**expert opinion**; not researched in this pass).
- **Training with the actual pack:**
  - from about **8 weeks out**, do one long hike per week with the full pack, packed as for the trip
  - progress from 8 kg to 12 kg, and to 13–14 kg on a few sessions to build margin
  - include ≥500–1,000 m of descent where terrain allows
  - this exposes hot spots from the hip belt and shoulder straps, blisters and pole technique under realistic conditions
  (Inference; **moderate** for descent pre-conditioning, **expert opinion** for the rest.)

### Gaps
- No RCT or prospective cohort in recreational hikers links a specific % of body weight to injury rates.
- No retrieved source quantifies the ideal hip-belt load share.

## 8. GR5-specific advice from reputable sources (GTA, FFRandonnée, NKBV, guide companies)

### Takeaway
GR5-specific sources agree on several points:
- the season is **June–September**, with snow risk on high cols until about end of June
- refuges open **mid-June to mid-September**
- huts should be **booked ahead in July–August**
- the Vanoise has the highest passes, at about 2,760–2,800 m
- some short sections are exposed or cabled
I could not access the official GTA or FFRandonnée pages directly.

### Cited Findings
- NKBV (Royal Dutch Alpine Club) presents the GR5 Alps as two one-week blocks: Léman → Les Houches → Modane. It mentions long climbs and serious descents, a section with cables and metal brackets, Col de Chavière (about 2,800 m) as the high point of week 2, and **NKBV member discounts in most Alpine huts** — [NKBV blog](https://nkbv.nl/actueel/blog/de-gr5-dwars-door-de-franse-alpen.html) (**expert opinion**)
- Staffed refuges are generally open **mid-June to mid-September**; reservations are "vivement recommandée" (strongly recommended) in July–August. Refuges usually offer **demi-pension** (dinner, bed and breakfast). Before end of June some high cols may be snow-covered and need suitable gear; check with hut wardens or the **PGHM** (mountain rescue gendarmerie) — [Échappées Montagnardes FAQ GR5](https://www.echappeesmontagnardes.fr/gr5-grande-traversee-des-vosges-du-jura-et-des-alpes/faq-gr5-tout-savoir-pour-reussir-la-traversee-des-vosges-du-jura-et-des-alpes/); [Mollow GR5 Alpes](https://www.mollow.eu/article/gr5-alpes-itineraire-etapes-conseils); [Toporando GR5](https://toporando.fr/grande-traversee-des-alpes-gta-gr5/) (**expert opinion**)
- The official GTA itinerary has **36 stages**, and its high point is the Col de la Leisse at about 2,760 m — [Ultimate France GR5](https://www.ultimatefrance.com/hiking/french-alps/gr5); see also [Outdooractive: GR5 La grande traversée des Alpes](https://www.outdooractive.com/en/route/hiking-trail/hautes-alpes/gr-5-la-grande-traversee-des-alpes/800121838/) and [Savoie Mont Blanc: GR5](https://www.savoie-mont-blanc.com/en/walking/gr5-grande-traversee-des-alpes-5016148/) (**expert opinion**/tourism body)
- The northern GR5 (NL → BE → LU → FR) starts on broad flat roads in the Netherlands and Flanders, then builds through the Ardennes, Vosges and Jura. The Jura section follows the Franco-Swiss border before descending towards Lake Geneva — [Wikipedia NL: Wandelroute GR5](https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wandelroute_GR5); [Oppad: GR5 Hoek van Holland – Nice](https://www.oppad.nl/gr5-wandelroute) (**expert opinion**)

### Inferences
- For a "GR5 in stages" plan, the **northern sections** (Ardennes, Vosges, Jura) work well as **progressive build-up blocks**: lower altitude, more asphalt and forest track, less D+ per km. Asphalt kilometres were a blister risk factor (see section 4), so footwear and socks still need attention there.
- The **Alpine block** (Léman → Nice) is where the 1,000 m D+/D− days, altitude above 2,500 m and possible early-season snow come together. Schedule it for **early July to mid-September**, the window after snow melt and inside hut opening.
- Book Vanoise refuges well in advance for July–August stages.
- Carry a **lightweight weatherproof layer and warm layer even in midsummer**, given altitudes up to about 2,800 m (**expert opinion**).

### Gaps
- The official GTA site (grande-traversee-alpes.com) and FFRandonnée topo-guide pages did not come up in search, and I could not fetch them, so official stage-by-stage D+/D− and hut altitudes are not verified here.
- The NKBV kenniscentrum (knowledge-base) pages on preparation and pack lists were not found; only the NKBV GR5 blog was retrieved.
- No guide-company (e.g., SNP, Allibert, La Balaguère) specific fitness or preparation requirements for the GR5 were retrieved.
