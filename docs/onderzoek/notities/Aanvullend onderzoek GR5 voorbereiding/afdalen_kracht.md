# Downhill/eccentric preparation and strength exercise selection for a GR5 trekker who also runs

Context: a recreational adult preparing for the GR5 (about 600 km in stages, about 12 kg pack, about 1,000 m of climb and 1,000 m of descent on a typical day) and also building toward a marathon. Current training is mostly uphill, with almost no downhill or eccentric work. The athlete does 3 strength sessions a week in MacroFactor Workouts (Upper A, Lower A heavy legs, Upper B).

Evidence grades used below:
- **Strong:** several RCTs or a meta-analysis with a consistent result.
- **Moderate:** one or a few RCTs, or a meta-analysis with heterogeneity or small samples.
- **Weak:** observational, mechanistic or single small study.
- **Expert opinion:** coaching or clinical consensus.

## 1. Downhill/eccentric preparation: protocols, minimal dose, no-decline-treadmill options, how often to repeat

### Takeaway
A small amount of real downhill walking protects well against muscle damage from later long descents. The repeated bout effect (RBE) appears after one bout (moderate evidence). It lasts at least 3–6 weeks but had disappeared by 9 weeks in the classic study, so the athlete should get a downhill or eccentric stimulus at least every 2–4 weeks and keep a regular, progressive downhill block going in the final 8–12 weeks before the GR5. In the gym, eccentric work also gives some protection (moderate evidence), but movement-specific downhill walking with a pack is the most specific and best-supported stimulus.

### Cited Findings
- **Minimal effective dose (moderate):** 36 untrained young men. A 5-min downhill walk (−28% grade, 5 km/h, 10% body-mass load, about 110 m of descent) done 1 week before a 40-min downhill walk (about 900 m of descent) roughly halved the damage. Knee-extensor strength loss was −9.9% vs −19.2% in controls (49% less), CK was 47% lower and soreness 66% lower. A 5-min level walk did not protect. The authors conclude that a small volume of downhill walking is crucial preparation for trekking. — [Maeo et al. 2017, PLoS One](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5348007/)
- **Duration of protection (moderate, one older study):** Byrnes et al. 1985 had subjects do two 30-min downhill runs at −10° separated by 3, 6 or 9 weeks. Protection (less soreness, CK and myoglobin) was present at 3 and 6 weeks but gone at 9 weeks. The authors conclude that a single bout protects "up to 6 weeks". — [Byrnes et al. 1985, J Appl Physiol](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4055561/)
- Bontemps et al. 2020, a narrative review in Sports Medicine:
  - **RBE size and time limits:** protection disappeared after 9 weeks without eccentric work between bouts. Larger initial damage gives larger protection, and greater training volume gives greater protection. — [Bontemps et al. 2020, Sports Med (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7674385/)
  - **Other cited RBE durations:** RBE protection has been reported for up to 6 months in some contexts. This conflicts with the 9-week loss in Byrnes 1985, and duration probably depends on the size of the first bout. — [Bontemps et al. 2020 summary](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-020-01355-z)
  - **Gym eccentric preconditioning (moderate):** isolated eccentric preconditioning done 14 days before downhill running cut isokinetic torque loss by about 22%, CK rise by 79% and tenderness by 52% over the following 7 days. — [Bontemps et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7674385/)
  - **Cost of an unaccustomed descent:**
    - About −16% to −24% knee-extensor MVC (peaks immediately, recovers in 4–5 days).
    - Running economy impaired for up to 3 days.
    - About 7% higher O2 cost of level running after a downhill bout.
    - Trained runners recovered in about 72 h, untrained in about 96 h.
    - Typical lab downhill protocols: median −12% grade (range −10 to −15%), about 40 min.

    — [Bontemps et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7674385/)
  - **Progression and frequency:** introduce downhill sessions "progressively" to avoid maladaptation and injury. Studies used 1–5 sessions/week for 1–8 weeks, with 2–9 weeks between familiarisation and competition. The optimal frequency is not established. — [Bontemps et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7674385/)
- **Expert opinion (Uphill Athlete):**
  - Box step-downs are the gym eccentric "downhill" exercise in muscular-endurance (ME) blocks, alongside box step-ups and walking lunges. Use a smaller box on the landing side to reduce step-down distance under fatigue.
  - Progress by cutting rest between sets rather than adding load.
  - ME blocks run once a week for 8–16 weeks, after general (4–12 wk) and max-strength (8–12 wk) phases.
  - Neglecting eccentric/downhill training and rucking only on flat terrain are listed as critical mistakes.

  — [Uphill Athlete, Muscular endurance for mountain athletes](https://uphillathlete.com/strength-training/muscular-endurance-for-mountain-athletes/)
- **Expert opinion:**
  - Coaches recommend tempo squats with a 3–4 s lowering phase, single-leg eccentric step-downs, split squats with controlled lowering, and Bulgarian split squats.
  - Prior downhill exposure is described as the most effective known way to limit damage on later descents.

  — [Uphill Athlete, How to train for skiing](https://uphillathlete.com/skiing/how-to-train-for-skiing/)
- **Downhill walking also impairs sensorimotor control (weak/mechanistic):** prolonged eccentric contraction during simulated downhill walking disturbs the sensorimotor control loops needed for safe foot–ground interaction. This is relevant to fall/sprain risk late in a descent day. — [PMC10094178](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10094178/)

### Inferences
- **Practical downhill protocol (moderate evidence for the principle, expert opinion for the exact numbers):**
  1. **Start (weeks 1–2):**
     - One "intro" descent of 100–200 m of descent (about 5–10 min) with a light pack, or 2–3 min of stair descent.
     - Expect mild DOMS (delayed-onset muscle soreness) at most. That is the RBE "vaccination".
     - Schedule it 2–3 days before a key run, not the day before.
  2. **Build (weeks 3–8):**
     - One downhill exposure per week.
     - Add roughly 100–200 m of descent per week, and add pack weight in steps of 2–3 kg.
     - Progress toward 500–700 m of descent with 8–10 kg.
  3. **Specific phase (last 6–10 weeks before the GR5):**
     - Every 1–2 weeks, a long hike with 800–1,200 m of climb and of descent and the full 12 kg pack.
     - At least 2 back-to-back days to test recovery.
  4. **Maintenance:**
     - Never go more than about 3–4 weeks without a downhill or eccentric stimulus. Byrnes found protection at 6 weeks but none at 9, and gaps between bouts leave less margin.
     - A short 10–15 min stair-descent session or weighted step-downs every 1–2 weeks probably keeps the adaptation (weak evidence; inferred from Maeo and Byrnes).
- **Without a decline treadmill (Netherlands context):**
  - Stairwells, for example 10–20 floors down with a 5–10 kg pack, taking the lift up. 1 floor ≈ 3 m, so 30 floors ≈ 90–100 m of descent.
  - Dunes, dikes, bridges or parking garages for repeats.
  - Weighted lateral/forward step-downs from a 20–30 cm box.
  - Slow-eccentric squats and split squats.

  Stair descent is roughly as steep as the −28% grade in Maeo (inference).
- **Two-step weekly link:**
  - Lower A (heavy) gives the max-strength and eccentric base.
  - A separate weekend stair-descent or hike gives the specific stimulus.
- **The first bout causes the most damage.** Do it on a low-stakes week, never in the 7–10 days before a race or a key long run. Bontemps reports up to 3 days of impaired running economy and up to 5 days of strength loss.

### Gaps
- No RCT directly compares step-downs, eccentric step-ups, stair descent and downhill treadmill walking for trekking outcomes. Exercise choice beyond "real downhill walking" is expert opinion.
- No study found on the exact maintenance frequency needed to keep RBE protection over a multi-month trek build-up. "Every 2–4 weeks" is inferred from Byrnes 1985 (3/6/9-week intervals).
- The Maeo 2017 subjects were untrained young men walking on a −28% treadmill. Generalising to a 12 kg pack, older recreational adults and multi-day accumulation is an inference.
- The Nordic hamstring exercise is not a downhill-specific exercise. Downhill damage falls mainly on the knee extensors (quadriceps), so Nordics are for hamstring injury risk, not quadriceps protection (see section 2).

## 2. Strength exercise selection for loaded hiking and running (sets, reps, progression, injury prevention, running economy)

### Takeaway
- **Injury prevention (strong):** strength training reduces sports injuries in general, with a dose-response.
- **Running injuries (mixed):** exercise programmes for runners have mixed results, and benefit shows mainly when they are supervised or adhered to.
- **Running economy (strong-moderate):** heavy resistance training (≥80–90% 1RM, roughly ≤4–6RM) is the best-supported single method. Heavy + plyometric combined looks best.
- **Load carriage (moderate):** resistance + aerobic training improves loaded-march performance, and progressive load carriage itself adds the most.

Priority exercises:
- heavy bilateral or single-leg knee-dominant work (squat or split squat)
- step-ups and step-downs
- a hip hinge (RDL)
- bent-knee calf raises (soleus)
- Nordic hamstring curls (low volume)
- hip-abductor work
- foot "core" exercises
- brief plyometrics for the running goal

### Cited Findings
- **General injury prevention:**
  - **Strength training (strong for sports in general):** Lauersen 2014 (BJSM, RCT meta-analysis) found strength training cut injury rates by 68%, proprioceptive training by 45% and multi-component programmes by 34%. Stretching gave no significant reduction. The authors call strength training the "cornerstone" of injury prevention. — [Lauersen et al. 2014, BJSM](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/55b7ffebe4b0568a75e3316b/t/58331e36e58c627c2abd00b5/1479745080809/Br+J+Sports+Med-2014-Lauersen-871-7.pdf)
  - **Dose-response (moderate, few studies):** Lauersen 2018 (6 studies, 7,738 participants aged 12–40, 177 injuries) found strength training reduced acute and overuse injuries, with a dose-response. A 10% increase in strength-training volume reduced injury risk by more than 4 percentage points. — [Lauersen et al. 2018, BJSM](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/327150440_Strength_training_as_superior_dose-dependent_and_safe_prevention_of_acute_and_overuse_sports_injuries_A_systematic_review_qualitative_analysis_and_meta-analysis)
- **Runners specifically (moderate, mixed):**
  - A 2024 Sports Medicine meta-analysis (9 studies, 1,904 participants) found no significant reduction in running injury risk or rate from exercise-based programmes overall.
  - A post-hoc analysis of supervised programmes only did show lower injury risk, so compliance and supervision may be the key factor.

  — [Sports Med 2024, PMC11127851](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11127851/)
- **Foot-core training (moderate, single RCT):**
  - Taddei et al. 2020 (AJSM) randomised 118 recreational runners with 1-year follow-up. The foot-strengthening group was 2.42× less likely to get a running-related injury than a warm-up/stretching control group.
  - The difference appeared by month 4, and greater foot-strength gain correlated with longer time to injury.
  - Protocol: 1 supervised session/week plus home sessions about 3×/week.

  — [Taddei et al. 2020 summary](https://lermagazine.com/issues/february/foot-core-training-to-prevent-running-related-injuries-a-survival-analysis-of-a-single-blind-randomized-controlled-trial); [trial registration](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02306148)
- **Nordic hamstring exercise:**
  - **Injury reduction (strong):** a meta-analysis of about 8,459 athletes (15 studies, 525 injuries) found that programmes including the Nordic hamstring exercise reduce hamstring injury risk by up to 51%. — [van Dyk et al. 2019, BJSM (summary)](https://barcainnovationhub.fcbarcelona.com/blog/a-recent-study-suggests-that-the-nordic-hamstring-exercise-could-help-reduce-ischiosural-muscle-injuries/); [CSP summary](https://www.csp.org.uk/frontline/article/nordic-hamstring-exercise-halves-hamstring-injury)

    Caveat: the evidence comes mainly from team sports with sprinting, and hamstring strain is less central for a trekker or marathoner (inference).
  - **Dose (moderate):** low-volume NHE improves eccentric strength about as much as high volume over ≥6 weeks (g = 2.28 vs 2.12). As few as 48 total reps improved strength. Low-volume start: 2 sets × 4 reps, 2×/week. — [Cuthbert et al. 2019 meta-analysis, PMC6942028](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6942028/); [Presland et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29572976/)
- **Running economy and performance:**
  - **Blagrove 2018 (strong):** systematic review of 26 studies. Strength training improved running economy by 2–8% vs control in most of the 20 studies that measured it. — [Blagrove et al. 2018, Sports Med](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29249083/); [full text](https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/210991205.pdf)
  - **Eihara 2022 (moderate-strong):** meta-analysis of 22 studies. Heavy resistance training (≥70% 1RM) beat plyometrics for running economy and time-trial performance. Near-maximal loads (≥90% 1RM, ≤4RM) and longer programmes gave the largest RE gains. — [Eihara et al. 2022, Sports Med Open (PMC9653533)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9653533/)
  - **Llanos-Lagos 2024 (moderate):** meta-analysis of 38 studies, 894 runners aged 17–40.
    - High-load training (≥80% 1RM) improved performance (ES −0.47).
    - Plyometrics alone did not reach significance (ES −0.21, p = 0.064).
    - Combined methods had the largest effect (ES −1.04).
    - There was no effect on VO2max; gains come through economy and neuromuscular factors.
    - Programmes ran 6–40 weeks at 1–4 sessions/week.

    — [Llanos-Lagos et al. 2024, Sports Med (PMC11258194)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11258194/)
- **Load carriage (moderate):**
  - Training effects were large (≥0.8 SD) when progressive resistance training was combined with aerobic training at least 3×/week for at least 4 weeks.
  - Effects were much larger (SES 1.7) when progressive load carriage was part of the programme.
  - Upper-body relative strength correlates more with loaded road-march performance than lower-body relative strength.
  - Load-carriage-specific sessions were recommended every 10–14 days.

  — [Knapik et al. 2012 systematic review, JSCR](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51842989_A_Systematic_Review_of_the_Effects_of_Physical_Training_on_Load_Carriage_Performance); [HPRC load-carriage strategies](https://www.hprc-online.org/physical-fitness/training-performance/load-carriage-strategies-improve-military-fitness); [Vaara et al. 2022, EJSS](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17461391.2021.1930193)
- **Hip abductors / glute med (moderate, mostly for treating patellofemoral pain rather than preventing it):**
  - Hip + knee strengthening reduces pain and improves function in patellofemoral pain better than knee strengthening alone. — [JOSPT 2018 meta-analysis](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2018.7365)
  - In distance runners, hip-abductor weakness is associated with ITB syndrome but unclear for PFPS (patellofemoral pain), MTSS (shin splints), tibial stress fracture or Achilles tendinopathy (weak). — [Systematic review, J Sci Med Sport](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S144024401630202X)
- **Calf/soleus (weak/biomechanical + expert opinion):**
  - The soleus produces roughly 6–8× body weight during running, and the gastrocnemius about 3×.
  - Coaching benchmarks:
    - about 30+ quality single-leg calf raises per leg
    - single-leg loaded calf-raise strength of about 1.6–2× body weight
  - Bent-knee (≥80° knee flexion) or seated calf raises target the soleus.

  — [Sportsmith](https://www.sportsmith.co/articles/training-lower-limb-for-performance-and-reduce-injury-risk/); [Trail Run Mag](https://www.trailrunmag.com/2020/01/14/calf-strength-for-runners/); [Recover Athletics](https://recoverathletics.com/soleus-strengthening-exercises-for-runners/). Mainly practitioner sources; the force figures come from musculoskeletal modelling studies not fetched here.

### Inferences
Concrete exercise menu for 2 lower-body sessions per week. The grade in brackets is the evidence behind each choice.

**Lower A (heavy legs), around 45–60 min:**
1. **Back squat, trap-bar deadlift or leg press:** 3–4 × 3–6 reps at about 80–90% 1RM (RIR 1–3), 2–3 min rest. [strong for running economy; Eihara, Blagrove, Llanos-Lagos]
2. **Bulgarian split squat or rear-foot elevated split squat:** 3 × 6–8 per leg with a 3–4 s lowering phase. [expert opinion for eccentric/downhill; moderate for single-leg strength transfer]
3. **Romanian deadlift:** 3 × 6–8. Or Nordic hamstring curl: 2 × 4–6, progressing to 3 × 6, 1–2×/week (low volume is enough). [strong for hamstring injury in sprint sports; weaker relevance here]
4. **Bent-knee (seated or Smith) calf raise:** 3 × 8–12, heavy. [weak/expert]
5. **Short plyometric block (on running-focused weeks):** 3 × 5–8 pogo hops or low box jumps at the start of the session, after warm-up. [moderate, combined method > single method]

**Second lower-relevant slot:** Upper B, or a short add-on after an easy run, 20–30 min:
1. **Weighted lateral/forward step-downs from a 20–30 cm box:** 3 × 8–12 per leg, 3–4 s lowering, progressing with a dumbbell or pack (+2–4 kg every 1–2 weeks). [expert opinion]
2. **Step-ups to a 40–50 cm box with pack or dumbbells:** 3 × 8–10 per leg. [expert opinion; load-carriage specificity]
3. **Hip-abductor work** (side plank with leg lift, banded lateral walk, cable/machine hip abduction): 2–3 × 12–15. [moderate for PFP treatment]
4. **Single-leg calf raise off a step, straight knee, slow eccentric:** 3 × 12–20 per leg. [weak/expert]
5. **Foot-core exercises** (short foot, toe curls/spreads, single-leg balance): 10 min, 2–3×/week. These can be done at home. [moderate, one RCT]
6. **Trunk** (suitcase carry, front/side plank, Pallof press): 2–3 sets. [expert opinion for pack carriage]

**Upper A/B (pack carriage):** keep rows, pull-ups or lat pulldowns and overhead pressing. Upper-body relative strength correlates with loaded-march performance (moderate, military data).

**Progression:**
- On heavy lifts, raise load when all sets reach the top of the rep range at RIR ≥2.
- On eccentric/accessory lifts, first lengthen the tempo or add reps, then add load.
- In the last 8–12 weeks before the GR5, shift Lower B toward muscular endurance: higher reps (12–20), shorter rest, step-ups/step-downs with pack weight (Uphill Athlete model), while Lower A keeps 1–2 heavy sets so max strength holds.

**Tibialis anterior:** tib raises, 2–3 × 15–25, are cheap insurance against anterior shin overload on descents and in early marathon training. This rests on expert opinion only; no outcome trial was found.

### Gaps
- No RCT tests a specific strength exercise selection against hiking/trekking injury or GR5-like performance. Selection for trekkers is extrapolated from military load carriage, running and team-sport injury data.
- Tibialis anterior strengthening: no trial found linking it to injury reduction in runners or hikers. Expert opinion only.
- Trunk/core training and backpack carriage: no direct trial found. Military data show upper-body relative strength correlates with march performance, but no causal trial on "core" work was found.
- Calf/soleus strength benchmarks (2× BW, 30 reps) are practitioner norms, not validated injury thresholds.
- Lauersen 2014/2018 effect sizes come from few trials, mostly in team sports and youth. The size of the benefit for recreational endurance athletes is uncertain, and the runner-specific 2024 meta-analysis found no overall effect.

## 3. Fitting this into 2 lower-body-relevant sessions/week and timing around runs and long hikes

### Takeaway
Concurrent strength and running training is compatible. Interference is small for max strength and hypertrophy, and mainly affects explosive strength when both are done in the same session or close together, especially with running (moderate evidence). So:
- put heavy Lower A on a day with no run, an easy run only, or a run done 6 or more hours earlier
- keep Lower A at least 48 h before long runs, intervals and long hikes, and at least 24 h after them
- treat long downhill hikes as a "leg session" in their own right

### Cited Findings
- Interference is more pronounced when strength and endurance are done in the same session than when separated by at least 3 h. Practitioners recommend at least 6 h between them. Running causes more interference than cycling. — [Wilson et al. 2012 meta-analysis, JSCR (PDF)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51719597_Concurrent_Training_A_Meta-Analysis_Examining_Interference_of_Aerobic_and_Resistance_Exercises)
- An updated meta-analysis found concurrent training does not compromise hypertrophy or maximal strength, but may blunt explosive strength, especially in the same session. Trained subjects saw no strength interference when sessions were split. — [Schumann et al., sportRxiv preprint (later Sports Med 2022)](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/37/71/54); [Stronger by Science summary](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/research-spotlight-interference-effect/)
- Intra-session order matters: a meta-analysis found sequencing affects lower-body strength outcomes in the interference effect. — [Eddens et al. 2018, PMC5752732](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5752732/)
- A novel downhill bout impairs running economy for up to 3 days and knee-extensor strength for 4–5 days. Trained subjects recover in about 72 h, untrained in about 96 h. — [Bontemps et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7674385/)
- Load-carriage-specific sessions every 10–14 days are recommended in military conditioning, alongside resistance and aerobic training. — [HPRC](https://www.hprc-online.org/physical-fitness/training-performance/load-carriage-strategies-improve-military-fitness)

### Inferences
**Example week** (consistent with the app's 48-h rule between leg-heavy sessions):

| Day | Session |
|---|---|
| Mon | Lower A (heavy), after an easy run in the morning or no run |
| Tue | Upper A + easy run |
| Wed | Intervals / hill run |
| Thu | Upper B + Lower B "lite" (step-downs, calves, hip, foot core; 20–30 min, low fatigue) or an easy run |
| Fri | Rest / easy |
| Sat | Long run **or** long hike with descent (alternate) |
| Sun | Easy recovery / rest |

**Rules:**
- Heavy lower work goes ≥48 h before the long run or hike and the interval session, and ≥24–48 h after a big descent day.
- After the first big descent, or a large jump in descent volume, allow about 3–4 days before quality running (Bontemps).
- On running quality days, run first and lift later (≥6 h apart if possible), or lift on the same day as an easy run. Do not do heavy squats right before intervals.
- In marathon-specific blocks, cut Lower A to maintenance: 1–2 heavy sets of 3–5 reps, plus plyometrics, with no extra eccentric volume. Avoid DOMS in the 10 days before a marathon.
- In GR5-specific blocks, swap the long run every other week for a long pack hike with descent. Lower B becomes the eccentric/ME session.
- Taper: stop new eccentric stimuli in the last 7–10 days before the GR5 start, but keep a short familiar descent session 7–14 days out so the RBE is fresh (inferred from the Maeo 1-week and Byrnes 3–6-week intervals).

### Gaps
- No studies were found on optimal sequencing of strength, running and loaded hiking specifically for combined trekking + marathon goals. The weekly layout is expert inference.
- Interference data come mostly from younger or trained participants. Recreational adults may see less interference (the Schumann preprint reports none in untrained subjects).

## 4. Mobility, stretching and dynamic warm-up evidence

### Takeaway
Static stretching does not reduce overall injury risk (moderate-strong), and has no meaningful effect on running economy acutely or chronically (moderate). Long holds (>60 s) just before performance can slightly reduce strength or power, while short holds (<60 s) inside a dynamic warm-up are harmless. Use a brief dynamic warm-up for performance. Stretch only where a specific range-of-motion need exists, or for comfort.

### Cited Findings
- Stretching interventions did not significantly reduce sports injuries (Lauersen 2014, RCT meta-analysis), while strength training did. — [Lauersen et al. 2014](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/55b7ffebe4b0568a75e3316b/t/58331e36e58c627c2abd00b5/1479745080809/Br+J+Sports+Med-2014-Lauersen-871-7.pdf)
- Behm et al. 2016 (APNM review):
  - Static stretching has no overall effect on all-cause or overuse injury.
  - It may reduce acute muscle injuries in sprint- and running-type activity.
  - Holds >60 s are more likely to cause small or moderate performance decrements; <60 s is generally fine.
  - Dynamic stretching after static stretching negates the decrements.

  — [Behm et al. 2016, APNM](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/10.1139/apnm-2015-0235); [NASM summary](https://blog.nasm.org/fitness/is-static-stretching-the-best-strategy-for-injury-prevention-and-performance-enhancement)
- A 2025 meta-analysis (17 studies: 15 acute, 2 chronic) found stretching did not change running economy acutely (overall ES 0.25, p = 0.21; static ES 0.33; dynamic ES 0.21). Two old chronic studies (3–10 weeks) also showed no effect. Conclusion: "current evidence does not support any effect of stretching on RE". — [Warneke et al. 2025, PMC12122984](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12122984/)
- The foot-core RCT used a warm-up + full-body stretching programme as the control, and that group had 2.42× higher injury likelihood than the foot-strength group. This is indirect evidence that stretching alone is an inferior prevention strategy. — [Taddei et al. 2020](https://lermagazine.com/issues/february/foot-core-training-to-prevent-running-related-injuries-a-survival-analysis-of-a-single-blind-randomized-controlled-trial)
- Dynamic warm-ups improved strength, speed and agility in reviews, while static stretching improved flexibility and sometimes impaired explosive output. — [Sople et al. 2025, Arthroscopy Sports Med Rehabil](https://arthroscopyjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.asmr.2024.101023)

### Inferences
- **Warm-up before runs or lifting:** 5–10 min easy jog or walk, then leg swings, walking lunges, A-skips, calf hops and 2–3 progressive strides or ramp-up sets. Static stretching is optional, kept <60 s per muscle, and should not be the main injury-prevention tool.
- **Time budget:** time is better spent on strength (calves, hips, foot core, eccentric quads) than on stretching routines.
- **Ankle dorsiflexion:** mobility for loaded descents and step-downs is reasonable to train if limited, for example knee-to-wall. This is expert opinion; no trial was found.

### Gaps
- No trials were found on stretching or mobility specifically for trekking or load-carriage injury outcomes.
- The chronic stretching → running economy evidence is only 2 old, small studies.
