# Marathon preparation for a recreational adult currently training for a GR5 trek

Context for the writer: the user currently runs ~2x/week (easy 30-40 min + long 50-70 min, plus weekly hill intervals), roughly 90-110 min/week, which at an easy recreational pace (~6:00-7:00 min/km) is about 14-20 km/week. The ASCEND program adds a second easy run in phase 2 (→ 3 runs/week). Evidence grades used below: **Strong** (meta-analysis/large RCTs or very large consistent cohorts), **Moderate** (single large cohort / smaller RCTs / consistent observational data), **Weak** (small studies, case studies, low-quality reviews), **Expert opinion** (coaching literature, plan conventions).

Search budget note: PubMed and several publisher pages (Sage, Springer, JOSPT) blocked direct fetching; some abstracts were obtained via Europe PMC's API or via search-result snippets. Where a number came only from a secondary summary, that is flagged.

## 1. Minimum and recommended weekly volume, runs/week and longest long run for a first marathon

### Takeaway
Total running volume (km/week, hours/week, number of long runs) is the most consistent training predictor of marathon time; for injury risk, averaging ≥30 km/week in the build is associated with roughly half the injury risk of <30 km/week. Mainstream novice plans use 4 runs/week, ~18 weeks, and a peak long run of ~32 km (20 mi) — though the 32 km figure is convention, not an evidence-derived threshold.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate–Strong, performance]** Doherty et al. 2020 meta-analysis with meta-regression (J Sci Med Sport) used average weekly running distance, number of weekly runs, maximum weekly distance, number of runs ≥32 km in the pre-marathon block, average training pace, longest run distance and hours of running per week as covariates; univariate meta-regression found a negative association between every training variable and marathon finish time (R² 0.38–0.81, p<0.001) — i.e., more of each = faster. — [PubMed 31704026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31704026/); [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/An-evaluation-of-the-training-determinants-of-A-Doherty-Keogh/40b42525e8b982b25f085123ff8ce39646bff072)
- **[Moderate]** Same meta-analysis: pooled non-finisher rate in the marathon was 7.27% (95% CI 6.09–8.65%). — [PubMed 31704026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31704026/) (via search snippet; direct fetch blocked)
- **[Weak — secondary source]** Doherty's analysis reportedly pooled ~85 papers and ~9,000 runners, with total volume as the most reliable predictor. — [Medium summary](https://medium.com/@cailbhe/the-easy-miles-paradox-why-slowing-down-makes-you-faster-46031677a6a9) (aggregator; sample size not verified against the paper)
- **[Moderate, very large observational]** Muniz-Pumares, Hunter, Meyler, Maunder & Smyth (Sports Med 2025; 151,813 marathons by 119,452 Strava runners, 16 weeks pre-race): the fastest runners accumulated >3× more training volume than the slowest; strong negative correlations between finish time and volume metrics. — [Sports Medicine 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-024-02137-7) (abstract via Europe PMC)
- **[Moderate, injury]** Rasmussen et al. 2013 (IJSPT; retrospective cohort of 662 marathon finishers, 68 injured = 10.3%): relative risk of injury was 2.02 (95% CI 1.26–3.24) for runners averaging <30 km/week vs those running 30–60 km/week; no further risk reduction above 60 km/week. Authors suggest a **minimum of 30 km/week** before a marathon. — [ResearchGate (Rasmussen 2013)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236207919_Weekly_running_volume_and_risk_of_running-related_injuries_among_marathon_runners)
- **[Moderate]** In the same study, injury risk was **not** associated with the longest training distance in the 6 weeks before the marathon, nor with sex or BMI. — [ResearchGate (Rasmussen 2013)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236207919_Weekly_running_volume_and_risk_of_running-related_injuries_among_marathon_runners)
- **[Expert opinion]** Hal Higdon Novice 1 and Novice 2 marathon plans: 18 weeks, 4 running days/week (three midweek runs + one long run), peak long run 20 miles (32 km), cutback weeks, ~3-week taper; Novice 1 prerequisite "run about 5 km comfortably", Novice 2 for runners with an existing base. — [yourmarathonpace.com summary of Higdon plans](https://yourmarathonpace.com/hal-higdon-marathon-training-plan); [Hal Higdon Novice 1](https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/marathon-training/novice-1-marathon/)
- **[Expert opinion]** Higdon recommends being at ~15–25 miles/week (≈24–40 km/week) before starting a marathon plan; week-1 long run of Novice 1 is 6 miles (~10 km). — [TrainingPeaks / Higdon](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/weekly-mileage-before-starting-a-marathon-training-program/); [Hal Higdon Novice base training](https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/base-training/novice-base-training/)

### Inferences
- Practical evidence-informed floor for a safe first marathon: **≥4 runs/week, average ≥30 km/week across the build, peaking ~45–55 km/week, with 2–3 long runs of 28–32 km (or ~2:45–3:00 h, whichever comes first)**. The 30 km/week floor is the best-supported number (Rasmussen); the peak and long-run numbers are plan convention (expert opinion), not trial-derived.
- Because Doherty's associations are cross-sectional (faster runners train more), they show "more volume ≈ faster", not a minimum safe dose; they should not be read as causal thresholds.
- The user's current ~15–20 km/week on 2 runs is roughly half the Rasmussen floor and below Higdon's entry point.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve Doherty's full text to extract per-variable slopes (e.g., minutes saved per extra km/week or per ≥32 km run). No RCT establishes a minimum long-run distance for completion.
- Time-capped long runs (e.g., 3 h max) for slower runners are a common coaching rule but I found no primary evidence comparing them to distance-capped long runs.

## 2. Realistic timeline from a ~2 runs/week, ~90–110 min/week base

### Takeaway
Standard novice plans are 16–20 weeks (Higdon: 18) and assume an entry base of ~24–40 km/week with a ~10 km long run. From ~15–20 km/week on 2 runs, a realistic path is ~8–12 weeks of base building first, then an 18-week plan — roughly **6–8 months** to a first marathon, with longer being safer for a novice.

### Cited Findings
- **[Expert opinion]** Higdon's 12-week Novice base-training program is explicitly designed to prepare runners to start the 18-week Novice marathon plan, starting with a ~3-mile run and finishing at a 6-mile long run and 15 miles/week — exactly week 1 of the marathon plan. — [Hal Higdon novice base training](https://www.halhigdon.com/training-programs/base-training/novice-base-training/)
- **[Expert opinion]** Higdon's "Novice Supreme" offers a 30-week ramp (first long run 3 miles), on the rationale that more time means better preparation and less overtraining-related injury. — [Hal Higdon marathon training](https://www.halhigdon.com/training/marathon-training/) (via search snippet)
- **[Moderate]** Novice runners have ~8× the injury incidence of recreational runners (33.0 vs 4.2 injuries per 1000 h of running) in the Correia 2024 umbrella review — an argument for a slower ramp when the base is small. — [Correia et al. 2024, J Sport Health Sci (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11336318/)
- **[Moderate]** Novices who increased weekly distance by >30% over a 2-week period were more vulnerable to distance-related injuries (874 novice runners, GPS-tracked), though overall injury rates did not differ significantly between progression groups. — [Nielsen et al. 2014, JOSPT (abstract via Europe PMC)](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2014.5164)

### Inferences
- Worked example from the user's base: at ~15–20 km/week with a 50–70 min long run (~8–11 km), the user is near Higdon's week-1 long run but below his weekly-volume entry point. Phase 2 of ASCEND (3 runs/week) moves toward ~25 km/week. A 4th run and ~25–30 km/week sustained for several weeks would make an 18-week plan start reasonable.
- Timeline: (a) ~8–12 weeks base to 25–30 km/week and 4 runs, then (b) 16–20 weeks marathon-specific (incl. 2–3 week taper) ⇒ **~24–32 weeks**. If the 16-week trek program itself raises running to ~25 km/week on 3 runs, the base phase can shrink to ~4–6 weeks after the trek.
- Using the Nielsen/Frandsen data, ramp rates of roughly ≤10% per long run (vs longest run in the last 30 days) and avoiding >30% two-week jumps support a slower, not faster, build.

### Gaps
- No trial compares, e.g., 12- vs 18- vs 24-week marathon builds in novices for injury or completion. Timeline numbers are expert opinion combined with injury-progression evidence.

## 3. Key session types, progression and intensity distribution

### Takeaway
For recreational marathoners the bulk of training should be easy (≈75–80%+ of time in zone 1), with some threshold/tempo and marathon-pace work; a pyramidal distribution is what >80% of the fastest Strava marathoners do, and the only marathon-specific RCT (2026) found no performance difference between polarized and pyramidal at matched load. Volume and long runs matter more than the exact intensity split.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate, very large observational]** Muniz-Pumares et al. 2025 (119,452 runners, 3-zone model): pyramidal TID was the most common approach and was adopted by >80% of runners with the fastest marathon times; higher proportions of time in higher-intensity zones correlated with slower finish times. — [Sports Medicine 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-024-02137-7) (abstract via Europe PMC)
- **[Weak–Moderate, small RCT]** Esteve-Lanao, Casado, Arévalo-Chico, Sellés-Pérez & Cejuela (Int J Sports Sci Coach, online 31 July 2026): 20 recreational marathoners, 16-week program, randomized to polarized (POL: 82/8/10% in zones 1/2/3) or pyramidal (PYR: 75/22/3%), weekly load matched. No significant between-group difference in marathon time (POL 3 h 45 min vs PYR 3 h 46 min, p=0.23) or simulated half-marathon improvement; PYR improved running economy more, POL showed a smaller second-half slowdown. — [SAGE journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/17479541261468168) (abstract via search snippets; direct fetch blocked)
- **[Weak — treat with caution]** A 2025 Scientific Reports paper reports 120 recreational marathoners randomized to 16 weeks of pyramidal vs polarized training, with polarized improving marathon time more (11.3 vs 8.7 min, p<0.03) and four "responder" clusters (polarized responders 31.5%, pyramidal 31.9%, dual 18.7%, non-responders 17.9%). — [Scientific Reports 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-25369-7); [PubMed 41286008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41286008/). Note: this is a machine-learning-framed paper; its claims were not independently verified and conflict with the null Esteve-Lanao RCT and with the Strava observational data.
- **[Expert opinion]** Novice plans structure the week as several easy midweek runs plus one long run with progressive build and cutback weeks; Higdon Novice 1 has no formal speedwork. — [yourmarathonpace.com summary](https://yourmarathonpace.com/hal-higdon-marathon-training-plan)

### Inferences
- Recommended session menu for a first-timer, roughly in priority order: (1) weekly long run, building ~1–3 km per week with every 3rd–4th week a cutback; (2) easy runs (conversational) to build frequency/volume; (3) one quality session/week — threshold/tempo (e.g., 2–3 × 8–12 min at "comfortably hard") in the base phase, shifting to marathon-pace segments inside long runs (e.g., last 5–10 km at goal pace) in the final 6–8 weeks; (4) strides. The existing weekly hill intervals can serve as the quality session during the trek block. (Expert-opinion synthesis; Daniels/Pfitzinger-style structure not directly fetched.)
- Polarized vs pyramidal is a second-order choice; for a novice whose goal is finishing, "mostly easy + one moderate session" is sufficient and consistent with both models.

### Gaps
- Could not fetch Daniels' Running Formula or Pfitzinger's Advanced Marathoning texts; specific pace prescriptions (e.g., Daniels T-pace ≈ 1-hour race pace) are from general knowledge, not verified here.
- Full text of Esteve-Lanao 2026 (weekly volumes, injury outcomes) not accessible.

## 4. Taper, race-day fueling and pacing

### Takeaway
Taper: ~2–3 weeks, cutting volume ~40–60% while keeping intensity and frequency; Strava data show a strict (consecutive) 3-week taper is associated with ~2.6% (~5.5 min) faster finishes than a minimal taper. Fueling: ~60–90 g carbohydrate/hour for most marathoners, trained in long runs beforehand.

### Cited Findings
- **[Strong]** Bosquet et al. 2007 meta-analysis (Med Sci Sports Exerc; 27 studies of swimmers, cyclists, runners): most effective taper ≈ 2 weeks with training volume reduced exponentially by 41–60%, maintaining intensity and frequency; average performance gain ~2.2%. — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Effects-of-tapering-on-performance:-a-Bosquet-Montpetit/a41517ab5fa06b92568b861e2b1aa32b3003d214); [Coaching Science Abstracts summary](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol131/bosquet.htm)
- **[Moderate, very large observational]** Smyth & Lawlor 2021 (Front Sports Act Living; 158,117 recreational marathoners on Strava, 2014–2017): ~90% used 2- or 3-week tapers but only 31% were "strict" (consecutive down-weeks). Versus a relaxed 1-week taper, a strict 3-week taper gave a median finish-time saving of 5 min 32 s (2.6%); strict 2-week: 2.14% (men) / 3.12% (women); strict 3-week: 2.38% (men) / 3.19% (women). Women benefited more. — [Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.735220/full)
- **[Moderate]** A more recent endurance-athlete taper meta-analysis exists (PLOS ONE 2023) — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0282838) (not fetched; numbers not extracted)
- **[Moderate / expert consensus]** For most marathoners the carbohydrate intake "sweet spot" is 60–90 g/h; >90 g/h is mainly for elites or well-practised runners. In training: 30–60 g/h for 60–90 min runs, 60–90 g/h for 1.5–2.5 h runs. — [RunnersConnect gut training](https://runnersconnect.net/marathon-gut-training/); [Precision Hydration](https://www.precisionhydration.com/performance-advice/nutrition/how-much-carbohydrate-carbs-athletes-per-hour/) (secondary coaching sources)
- **[Weak–Moderate]** Gut training: a 2017 Monash University study found runners practising 90 g/h for 2 weeks had fewer GI symptoms and better carbohydrate tolerance. — [Science in Sport](https://www.scienceinsport.com/sports-nutrition/inside-the-science-of-gut-training/) (secondary; primary = Costa et al. 2017, not fetched)
- **[Weak]** Most runners reportedly consume only ~21 g carbohydrate/hour, well below recommendations. — [RunnersConnect](https://runnersconnect.net/marathon-gut-training/) (secondary, primary source unclear)
- **[Weak–Moderate]** In a trail marathon study, 120 g/h vs 60 and 90 g/h was associated with less neuromuscular impairment and better recovery of high-intensity capacity. — [PMC7400827](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7400827/)

### Inferences
- Practical taper for this user: start reducing volume 3 weeks out (e.g., ~80% → ~60% → ~40% of peak week incl. race), keep run frequency and short bits at marathon pace/threshold; down-weeks must be consecutive ("strict").
- Fueling: practise 40–60 g/h on every long run >90 min, progress to 60–90 g/h in the last 6–8 weeks, using exactly the race-day products.
- Pacing: the Esteve-Lanao RCT used second-half slowdown as an outcome, which supports even pacing as the target; a conservative first-half (even or slightly negative split) is standard expert advice.

### Gaps
- No specific primary data fetched on pacing strategies for novice marathoners (e.g., Smyth's Strava pacing/"hitting the wall" analyses exist but were not retrieved within the search budget).
- Fluid/sodium guidance not researched.

## 5. Combining with the GR5 trek goal: priority, conflicts, transfer, order and recovery

### Takeaway
There is no direct research on sequencing a multi-day trek and a first marathon. Evidence on specificity and injury risk supports **trek first, then marathon**: the current 16-week program already builds an aerobic and leg-strength base, hiking does not substitute for running-specific volume, and running a marathon build simultaneously with heavy pack/hike volume would stack leg-heavy load. Allow roughly 1–2 weeks of easy recovery after the trek before starting a marathon-specific build.

### Cited Findings
- **[Weak, case study]** A 768-km, 11-day multi-stage ultra-trail run: lower-limb strength fell 28–36% immediately post-race and returned to baseline by day 9; CK rose ~888% and normalized by day 9. — [PMC8776162](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8776162/) (far more extreme than a hiking trek; upper-bound illustration only)
- **[Weak]** Downhill walking and mountain trekking induce muscle damage requiring several days of recovery; fatigue and foot pain on multi-day hikes often appear from day 1 and persist. — [PMC5348007 (downhill walking muscle damage)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5348007/)
- **[Weak]** Multi-day (4–5 day) hiking measurably affects postural control. — [PLOS ONE: Effects of Four Days Hiking on Postural Control](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0123214)
- **[Weak]** Trekking poles reduced muscle soreness at 24 and 48 h after a trek and sped recovery. — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/06/100602121000.htm) (press summary)
- **[Expert opinion]** Cross-training added on top of running tends to help; replacing running with cross-training can go either way. Hiking builds hip/leg strength and proprioception but is lower intensity and impact and does not replicate running-specific economy. — [Marathon Handbook cross-training](https://marathonhandbook.com/cross-training-for-runners/); [Trail Runner: science of cross training](https://www.trailrunnermag.com/training/the-science-of-cross-training-to-build-trail-running-fitness/)
- **[Moderate]** Correia 2024: "participation in other sports" is listed among risk factors for running injuries, and marathon participation and higher weekly distance are associated with injury (but all 13 included reviews were low or critically-low quality). — [Correia et al. 2024 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11336318/)

### Inferences
- **Priority/order:** Keep the GR5 trek as the primary goal for the current 16-week program. Running stays at 2–3 runs/week as aerobic and durability support. After the trek: ~1–2 weeks of easy activity (longer if there are aches or tendon pain), then 4–8 weeks to build to 4 runs/~30 km/week, then a 16–18 week marathon block. The marathon would then be ~6–7 months after the trek.
- **Why not marathon first or both together:** (a) a marathon peak needs ~45–55 km/week running plus long runs of 2.5–3 h, which competes for the same recovery as long weekend hikes with a pack; ASCEND's scheduler already treats hikes and lower-body sessions as "leg-heavy" with a 48 h spacing rule, so marathon long runs would compete for the same slots; (b) the novice injury rate is high, and adding a large new running load on top of hiking load increases total lower-limb stress; (c) specificity: hike/pack fitness and running fitness overlap only partly.
- **Does hiking count?** Partly. It builds aerobic base, time on feet, and eccentric leg strength (downhill), useful for long-run durability. It should **not** be counted 1:1 toward weekly running km for marathon readiness or the 30 km/week floor. A reasonable rule (expert opinion): count hikes as aerobic training time/load, but not as running km or long runs.
- **Positive carry-over after the trek:** a multi-day trek gives a large aerobic and durability stimulus; once recovered, the user starts the marathon build fitter than their running km suggests. But running-specific tissue (tendons, bone) still needs a gradual ramp, so the spike rules in §6 still apply.
- **If the user insists on a marathon within ~3 months after the trek:** only realistic if running was kept at ≥3 runs/~25 km/week throughout the trek program; otherwise the timeline in §2 applies.

### Gaps
- No studies were found on (a) a marathon build after a multi-day trek, (b) how hiking volume transfers to marathon performance, or (c) recovery time after a typical ~1–3 week GR5-type trek (~20–25 km/day with a pack). The 1–2 week recovery recommendation is an inference from muscle-damage time courses (days to ~9 days) and is expert opinion only.

## 6. Injury risk in marathon training for novices and mitigation

### Takeaway
Novice runners get injured about 8× as often per hour as experienced recreational runners. The strongest modifiable signals are sudden spikes: a single run more than 10% longer than the longest run in the last 30 days raises injury rate (HRR 1.5–2.3), and >30% increases in weekly distance over 2 weeks raise distance-related injuries in novices. Averaging <30 km/week before a marathon roughly doubles injury risk.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate — large cohort]** Frandsen et al. 2025 (BJSM; 5,205 runners, 18 months, Garmin data, 588,071 sessions): 1,820 (35%) had a running-related injury (72% overuse, 28% traumatic). A single session >10% longer than the longest run in the previous 30 days increased injury rate: small spikes (>10–30%) HRR 1.64, moderate (>30–100%) HRR 1.52, large (>100%) HRR 2.28. Week-to-week ratio: no association. ACWR: negative dose-response (higher ACWR ≈ lower rate). — [BJSM paper (SDU repository PDF)](https://findresearcher.sdu.dk/ws/files/295073070/1203.full.pdf); [MDSpire summary](https://news.mdspire.com/orthopedic-medicine/articles/study-defines-too-much-running-distance/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393493797_How_much_running_is_too_much_Identifying_high-risk_running_sessions_in_a_5200-person_cohort_study)
- **[Moderate]** Nielsen et al. 2014 (JOSPT; 874 GPS-tracked novices): progression >30% over a 2-week period was associated with more distance-related injuries; overall injury rate did not differ significantly between progression groups. — [JOSPT 2014](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2014.5164) (abstract via Europe PMC)
- **[Moderate]** Correia et al. 2024 umbrella review (J Sport Health Sci; 13 systematic reviews, 148 primary studies, 207 outcomes): incidence 33.0 injuries/1000 h in novice runners vs 4.2/1000 h in recreational runners. Effect sizes: 23% large, 29% medium, 37% small, 11% none. Large-effect categories: running/training characteristics (weekly distance, marathon participation, race frequency), health/lifestyle (previous injury, etc.), morphological/biomechanical factors (e.g., hip/pelvic stability, knee extension strength deficits). Evidence quality: 8 of 13 reviews critically low quality, 5 low, none moderate/high. — [Correia et al. 2024 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11336318/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2095254624000607)
- **[Moderate]** Rasmussen 2013: <30 km/week average → RR 2.02 for injury vs 30–60 km/week among marathon finishers; knee, foot and ankle most often affected. — [ResearchGate (Rasmussen 2013)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236207919_Weekly_running_volume_and_risk_of_running-related_injuries_among_marathon_runners)
- **[Weak–Moderate]** In 95 endurance athletes followed for 52 weeks, <7 h sleep and psychological/lifestyle health complaints in the prior 7 days were associated with new injury risk. — [Johnston et al. 2020, J Sci Med Sport (Europe PMC record)](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1016/j.jsams.2019.10.013&resultType=core&format=json)

### Inferences
- Concrete mitigation rules that ASCEND could encode in `engine/` (deterministic):
  1. **Long-run cap:** no single run >110% of the longest run in the previous 30 days (Frandsen). Strongest, most actionable rule.
  2. **2-week progression cap:** weekly running km should not rise >30% over any 2-week window (Nielsen) — in practice ~10–15%/week with a cutback week every 3–4 weeks.
  3. **Volume floor before race:** average ≥30 km/week across the build (Rasmussen); flag a marathon goal as "not yet realistic" below this.
  4. **Previous injury flag:** prior injury is one of the strongest risk factors (Correia), so slow the ramp after any injury.
  5. **Sleep/recovery:** surface low sleep (<7 h) as a caution signal (Johnston), fitting the readiness module.
  6. **Strength work:** hip/knee strength deficits appear among risk factors (Correia), supporting the program's existing strength sessions — though causal prevention evidence was not established here.
- Since the Frandsen rule is about single-session distance relative to recent maximum, a hike-heavy month does **not** "unlock" a long run — the reference should be the longest *run*, not the longest hike (inference; the study used running sessions only).

### Gaps
- Could not extract confidence intervals for Frandsen's HRRs (PDF unreadable in this environment) or Nielsen's hazard ratios (JOSPT page blocked).
- No marathon-specific RCT of injury-prevention strategies for novices was found within the search budget.
- The Correia review's evidence base is all low/critically-low quality — the risk factor list is hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.
